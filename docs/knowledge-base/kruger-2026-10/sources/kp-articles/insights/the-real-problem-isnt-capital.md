KP THOUGHT LEADERSHIP / CAPITAL & FUNDING

# The Real Problem Isn’t Capital. It’s Readiness.

*Before asking who will finance the business, establish whether the business can responsibly absorb finance.*

By The Business Clinic.Africa | 14 July 2026 | Pan-African business analysis  

## The pitch-deck illusion

Every funding season produces an impressive collection of pitch decks. Graphs rise elegantly. Markets expand obligingly. The promised returns are apparently immune to customers, competitors and the laws of arithmetic. Then the investor asks for a reconciled cash-flow forecast, signed customer contracts and evidence that the founders can deliver. The room becomes remarkably quiet.

Access to finance is a genuine constraint. The IFC reported in April 2025 that the global financing gap for micro, small and medium enterprises stood at an estimated US\$5.7 trillion, rising to US\$8 trillion when informal firms are included \[1\]. The estimate describes an unmet financing need; it does not prove that every enterprise is investment-ready or that available finance would be appropriate. Demand for capital and capacity to use capital are separate questions.

A lender is concerned about repayment. An equity investor is concerned about value creation, downside protection and a plausible exit or distribution path. A development financier may also assess jobs, inclusion or climate outcomes. Different mandates, same fundamental concern: can this enterprise turn resources into measurable results without creating an expensive surprise?

Consider a project developer with an excellent site and a persuasive market narrative. If land rights are uncertain, environmental approvals have not been mapped and the construction estimate lacks independent review, a bigger cheque does not close the gaps. It magnifies them. Financing may be unavailable because the capital stack is wrong, but it may also be unavailable because the project is not yet bankable. Confusing the two can waste a year.

## Five pillars that make capital usable

The first pillar is strategic clarity. Define one customer problem, who pays to solve it and why the firm can compete. A serviceable market is not the same as the total population multiplied by an optimistic price.

The second is financial discipline: a reliable opening balance sheet, monthly cash forecast, tax position and clear distinction between revenue, booked orders and cash collected.

The third pillar is governance. Decision rights, separation of duties, conflicts disclosures and reliable records signal whether management can safeguard someone else’s money.

The fourth is execution capacity. Show the named people, suppliers, systems and permissions needed to deliver on time.

The fifth is evidence. A pilot, repeat purchases, bank statements and independently checked demand are stronger than testimonials written by someone who has never bought the product.

The project-development lesson is painfully public. South Africa’s Auditor-General examined 152 national and provincial infrastructure projects for the 2024–25 audit cycle, with findings on 136 of them \[2\]. These findings are not a universal private-sector failure rate. They do, however, demonstrate why planning, contract management and oversight cannot be postponed until after money is committed.

Readiness is also specific to the instrument. A working-capital line requires a receivables and inventory cycle that can service debt. Long-lived infrastructure may require project finance with contracts, security and contingency. Innovation with uncertain early cash generation may require patient equity, grants or staged funding. A business can be promising and still be unsuitable for a particular financing product. The mistake is not rejection. The mistake is repeatedly applying for the same instrument without addressing the reason for rejection.

## Readiness is measurable, not rhetorical

A simple readiness score can use five categories; each graded from zero to four. Zero means there is no evidence; one means a claim has been drafted; two means internal evidence exists; three means an external party has validated it; and four means the proof remains valid under a tested downside case. Maximum score: twenty. This is an editorial diagnostic, not an accredited credit rating.

Require a source for each score. For strategy it may be verified customer interviews and pricing tests. For finance it may be reconciled accounts and signed-off forecasts. For governance, board minutes and an updated share register. For execution, permits and supplier capacity. For proof, payments and customer retention.

Do not allow a high aggregate score to conceal a fatal weakness. Missing land rights or an unresolved licence can block an infrastructure project despite excellent financial projections. The score starts a conversation; it does not override investment committee judgement.

## The thirty-day capital-readiness test

Week one: establish the truth. Reconcile twelve months of banking activity, identify overdue creditors, test receivables and separate actual revenue from pipeline. Week two: define the investment case. Produce a three-scenario model showing base, upside and downside demand, operating costs and cash runway. State which variables change the answer; do not hide them behind a colourful sensitivity chart.

Week three: test execution. Validate legal rights, permits, procurement, customer commitments, management capacity and delivery milestones. Assign a named owner to each gap. Week four: assemble a decision room: a short investment memorandum, data-room index, risk register, governance map and proposed use-of-funds schedule. Each funding tranche should unlock a verifiable milestone rather than reward an eloquent meeting.

A simple illustration shows why this matters. Suppose a firm seeks R10 million but cannot explain how the first R2 million will convert into contracts and collections. Granting the full R10 million on day one would only make the uncertainty more expensive. Releasing R2 million against defined evidence, with the balance conditional on progress, can align capital with delivery. The numbers are illustrative, not a market benchmark or funding recommendation.

The decisive question is therefore not “Who has money?” It is “What evidence would make this risk financeable, on terms the enterprise can sustain?” Ask the second question before paying another designer to animate a hockey-stick graph. Capital should accelerate a sound operating model, not subsidise an unresolved one.

## Boardroom takeaway

Before submitting a financing application, insist on five signed-off files: strategy, cash-flow model, governance register, execution plan and independent evidence. If one is missing, the immediate investment may need to be in readiness, not borrowing.
