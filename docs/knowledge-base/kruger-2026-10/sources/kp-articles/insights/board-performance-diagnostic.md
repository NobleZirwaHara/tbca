# Board Performance Diagnostic

*A board that asks better questions improves capital allocation, risk oversight and management accountability.*

By The BusinessClinic.Africa | 23 September2026 | Framework  

## Governance is behaviour, not furniture

**A board can have twelve experienced directors, a polished charter and very comfortable chairs, then still fail at its central task: making informed decisions about strategy, risk, leadership and the responsible use of capital. Board effectiveness cannot be inferred from meeting frequency, age of the institution or the thickness of the pack. The question is whether the board changes decisions and strengthens controls before avoidable losses become permanent.**

The G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance 2023 identify responsibilities including strategic guidance, effective monitoring of management, accountability and oversight of sustainability and resilience \[1\]. These are international principles that jurisdictions adapt through their own laws and listing rules. They are not a legal opinion about any specific South African, Kenyan or Nigerian entity.

A performance diagnostic should therefore investigate evidence from the last twelve months: major decisions, options considered, dissent recorded, risks escalated, management follow-through and outcomes after decisions. Avoid asking only whether directors “feel well informed.” People who have received 180 pages before every meeting may feel informed while knowing remarkably little about what is actually happening.

## Six dimensions that separate oversight from theatre

**Begin with strategy and capital allocation. Can the board explain which customers and projects the business prioritises, the expected benefit, the investment at risk and the circumstances under which funding should stop? Next test information quality. Are financial reports reconciled and timely? Can directors locate material changes in liquidity, concentration, complaints and control exceptions without searching through annexures?**

Third, examine risk and resilience: a current risk appetite, scenario planning, technology dependencies, crisis response and cyber incident escalation. Fourth, examine executive performance and succession, including whether the chief executive is assessed against the strategy and whether credible successors exist. Fifth, review board composition and independence: conflicts disclosed, relevant competence available and committee mandates functioning. Sixth, test meeting effectiveness: decisions recorded with owners and closure evidence.

The Basel Committee’s Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision, current from April 2024, emphasise bank governance, risk management, operational resilience and third-party dependencies \[2\]. Their direct regulatory application concerns banks and supervisors. Other boards can still learn from the discipline of mapping critical operations, testing disruption plans and reviewing technology concentrations; they should not claim that Basel rules automatically govern an ordinary trading company.

## Use a diagnostic with transparent evidence

**For each of the six dimensions, score from zero to four. Zero means the activity is absent. One means it is documented but not implemented. Two means it occurs inconsistently. Three means operating evidence exists over repeated cycles. Four means effectiveness has been independently tested and led to improvement. Maximum score: 24. This is The BusinessClinic.Africa’s editorial diagnostic scale, not a licensed professional standard. Record the source and reviewer beside each score.**

Do not hide critical failures under an average. A score of 21 out of 24 offers little comfort if the missing three points represent undisclosed related-party dealings, a liquidity crisis or absent regulatory permission. Mark red-line issues separately. The chair should treat a documented disagreement as an asset when it improves the decision; forced consensus often produces attractive minutes and poor judgement.

A practical evidence request contains ten items: board and committee mandates, rolling calendar, board packs, minutes and decision log, strategy and budget, risk register, management accounts, audit findings, conflict declarations, succession plan and crisis-testing records. Interview directors individually and management separately. Compare what the board thinks it instructed with what the organisation actually did.

## A ninety-day board improvement plan

**In the first month, gather evidence, interview participants and identify the three highest-consequence gaps. In month two, redesign the dashboard, clarify decision rights and set owners for overdue actions. In month three, run one disciplined meeting with explicit decision papers, challenge assumptions and check action closure. Repeat the score after two quarters, not the day after a new charter is approved.**

A good board paper identifies the decision required, alternatives, base and downside financial effect, material risks, stakeholder implications and why management recommends an option. It should fit the issue, not an arbitrary slide count. For routine matters, one page may be enough. For a complex acquisition or public infrastructure transaction, independent legal and financial analysis may be essential.

The board’s job is not to run operations or to offer supportive applause. It is to exercise informed judgement, protect the institution and test whether the promised outcomes are becoming real. If the chair ends every meeting with “excellent presentation” but no decision, the governance system may be highly courteous and operationally useless.

## The diagnostic meeting: questions directors should not avoid

Begin with liquidity. Can the board explain the company’s usable cash, committed facilities, near-term obligations and forecast under a downside case? Move to customers and concentration. What proportion of revenue or margin relies on the largest contracts, and how quickly could those contracts be replaced? Then review critical technology, operational and regulatory dependencies. A director need not be a software engineer to ask what happens when a payment platform fails for a full business day.

Examine the last three decisions that materially changed the business. For each, compare the original assumption with current evidence. Was the expected benefit realised? Was management held accountable? Did dissent or alternative options appear in the decision paper? The diagnostic is more credible if it reveals lessons from a wrong decision than if it produces a flattering aggregate score. Minutes are an audit trail, not a substitute for thought.

Conflicts of interest require explicit treatment. Document interests before discussion, follow applicable legal requirements and explain how affected directors were excluded or otherwise managed where necessary. Do not substitute personal confidence in a colleague for a controlled process. Also review information asymmetry: if management alone sets the assumptions, prepares the paper, judges the options and marks completion, the board may never see an independent challenge.

Close with a short action register containing no more than the most material improvements: the weakness, consequence, evidence, responsible person and date. Review whether action closure changed board behaviour. If the review produces only a new governance manual, the board may have improved its filing system without improving its judgement.

## Boardroom takeaway

**Require a named owner, a credible baseline, a measurable improvement and a decision date. Revisit the evidence before committing the next tranche of time, money or reputation.**
