# Digital Banking in Africa: The Next Leap

*The next advance will depend less on new apps and more on safe, interoperable, affordable and genuinely useful banking.*

By The Business Clinic.Africa | 09 August 2026 | Pan-African Business Analysis  

## Access expanded. Usefulness is the next test

**Digital banking is regularly presented as the moment every financial task moves onto a phone. Yet customers care less about the channel than whether their salaries arrive, merchants are paid, savings remain safe and mistakes can be corrected. For a small business, a service that connects payments, records, working capital and tax administration may be more useful than one with another elegant dashboard and an unfamiliar password rule.**

The World Bank’s Global Findex 2025 report found that 79% of adults worldwide held a financial account in 2024. For Sub-Saharan Africa the figure was 58%, up from 49% in 2021 \[1\]. These are household-survey measures and regional estimates, not estimates of active online banking customers. Their message is two-sided: account access has advanced, and substantial gaps remain. Neither the existence of an account nor a downloaded app demonstrates affordable, confident or frequent usage.

The World Bank also reported that among the four billion adults in low- and middle-income economies owning a mobile phone, around half used a password to secure it \[1\]. The implication is not that every phone is unsafe. It is that product design must assume uneven digital literacy and security habits. Faster onboarding is useful only if fraud prevention, assistance and recovery work for real users.

## From isolated products to financial journeys

The next leap is interoperability: an enterprise should be able to receive a payment, reconcile it, obtain a record and move funds through compliant channels without manually entering the same information into four systems. Open interfaces can help, but only where security, privacy, consent, liability allocation and operational service levels are sound. A poorly managed interface exports risk as efficiently as it exports data.

Global mobile-money activity illustrates the scale of connected financial behaviour. GSMA reported over US\$2.1 trillion in transactions during 2025, 2.3 billion registered accounts and 593 million active 30-day accounts \[2\]. These global figures are not bank deposits, and registered accounts are not unique account-holders. They should not be added to Findex ownership figures as if the populations were independent. The point is that payments have become an operating layer for millions of interactions.

A useful bank-fintech partnership should be judged by customer outcomes: settlement time, disputed payments resolved, fee transparency, small-merchant onboarding, availability outside major cities and measurable fraud rates. A new partnership announcement is an input. Reliable service six months later is an outcome.

## Trust is infrastructure too

The Basel Committee’s revised Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision, effective April 2024, explicitly address technological change, operational resilience, cyber risk and third-party dependencies \[3\]. The principles are directed to banking supervision and are not a substitute for each jurisdiction’s rules. They demonstrate why digital banking cannot be governed as a marketing feature. Outages, fraud, concentrated cloud providers and weak vendor controls can affect real people and financial stability.

Design for failure as well as success. Map critical services, authenticate customers proportionately, protect personal information, conduct incident exercises and maintain clear reversals and complaints processes. For complex credit products, explain total repayment obligations and affordability requirements. If customers need a forensic accountant to understand the fee schedule, the user experience has failed, even if the interface has won awards.

The cost of cross-border payments is another opportunity. The World Bank’s Remittance Prices Worldwide platform reported a global average cost of 6.36% for sending remittances in its 2025 reporting \[4\]. This is an observed global average across monitored corridors, not the cost of every African transfer. A fee reduction that is transparent and reaches actual consumers can produce measurable value, but must be assessed alongside foreign-exchange, compliance and service risks.

## Where boards should invest next

Focus on four capabilities. First, safe identity and recovery pathways that reduce exclusion without disregarding anti-money-laundering obligations. Second, interoperable payments and reconciliation tools for businesses. Third, transparent credit and savings products with tested consumer outcomes. Fourth, operational resilience: uptime, cyber controls, third-party monitoring and rapid complaint resolution. Each investment should have a baseline and a defined customer segment.

For a ninety-day pilot, select one merchant journey. Measure average settlement time, proportion of payments automatically reconciled, failed transactions, service complaints and the real cost to the merchant. Run the pilot with customers who have varied devices and connectivity. Do not extrapolate a successful pilot in a central-city office to the entire continent without testing the conditions that differ.

The next leap in African banking is not a competition to add the most features. It is the much more difficult business of making financial services reliable, fair, connected and useful. The customer may never admire the architecture. That is usually a sign that it works.

## The board’s five tests for digital-bank expansion

First, does the proposed product solve a measured problem? The relevant segment might be small traders needing low-cost payments, wage earners needing predictable access or rural customers requiring assisted onboarding. Define the service baseline: time, fee, failure rate and complaint experience. Second, do the economics work after security, compliance, distribution and customer support? Do not infer profitability from the total value passing through the platform.

Third, what does the worst credible operating day look like? Simulate unavailable authentication, a cloud-provider incident, corrupted payment instructions and a sudden surge in fraud attempts. Estimate maximum customer harm, legal obligations, recovery time and which executive can decide to suspend a service. Fourth, can customers exercise rights in practice: reverse an error, reach support, see the total cost and recover access when a device is lost? The control is not effective if it exists only in a policy file.

Fifth, is expansion equitable? Monitor access by region, age, disability-related needs and other lawful, relevant categories without unnecessarily collecting sensitive data. A digital service may reduce branch dependence but still exclude people who lack a device, stable power, identification or affordable connectivity. The World Bank’s Findex findings make clear that account ownership and mobile-phone ownership are distinct measures \[1\]. Build channels for actual use, not merely for accounts that look impressive in quarterly reporting.

Assign one management owner to each test, approve thresholds before the launch and review them after thirty and ninety days. If the bank cannot explain a failed payment to a customer clearly, it should not claim to have solved the future of finance. Reliability and fairness may sound unexciting beside a digital transformation slogan. They are the reasons customers return.

## Boardroom takeaway

**Require a named owner, a credible baseline, a measurable improvement and a decision date. Revisit the evidence before committing the next tranche of time, money or reputation.**
