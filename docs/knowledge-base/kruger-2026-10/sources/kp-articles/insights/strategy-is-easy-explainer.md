# Strategy is Easy. Execution is Unforgiving.

*A strategy becomes credible only when choices, resources, owners and customer outcomes meet in operating reality.*

By The BusinessClinic.Africa | 13 September 2026 | Pan-African Business Analysis  

## Planning is not the same as choosing

Boardrooms produce strategies at astonishing speed. They contain growth pillars, transformation priorities and ambitious slogans. The harder work begins when a manager asks which initiatives will be stopped, which customer segment will come first and who is accountable when a deadline slips. Strategy is a set of choices under constraint. If every opportunity is a priority, no opportunity has been prioritised.

The Project Management Institute’s 2024 Pulse of the Profession study reported an average performance rate of 73.8% among the surveyed organisations’ completed projects, where performance meant the self-reported share meeting business goals \[1\]. It also reported that teams working with predictive, hybrid and agile methods could achieve similar performance, suggesting that fit-for-purpose management and organisational support matter more than worshipping one delivery method.

In South African public infrastructure, the Auditor-General’s 2024–25 national and provincial review found issues on 136 of 152 projects sampled \[2\]. These findings cannot be transplanted into a failure rate for corporate strategy. Their relevance is the pattern of delivery challenges: weak planning, procurement and oversight can interrupt the conversion of approved intention into useful services.

A credible strategy therefore needs three linked documents, not one glossy presentation. The first is a decision thesis: where to compete, why customers will choose us and what we will not do. The second is an operating plan: named owners, sequence, capacity and dependencies. The third is a measurement contract: the few outcomes that show whether strategic choices are working. Most teams possess the first. Some possess the second. Very few maintain the third once the launch conference ends.

## The execution architecture

Build a portfolio of no more initiatives than the enterprise can staff and finance. For each initiative, define a sponsor who can allocate resources and an accountable delivery owner who controls day-to-day progress. Set entry criteria, stage gates, budget exposure and evidence of benefits. A target like “grow regional market share” is not actionable until it names the segment, route to market, investment and measurement period.

Manage dependencies explicitly. A digital service launch may depend on verified customer data, payments integration, cyber review and trained frontline employees. The software team cannot “work harder” to compensate for a missing legal authority to collect data. Dependencies should have owners, dates and escalation rights. Treat unresolved dependencies as risks, not as small-print footnotes.

Use a weekly performance forum to distinguish three measures. Activities tell you what people did. Outputs tell you what was delivered. Outcomes tell you whether the work created customer or enterprise value. Training thirty employees is an activity; demonstrating proficiency is an output; reducing errors or turnaround time is an outcome. Conflating these creates a business that is busy, applauded and still underperforming.

Sequence investment through learning. Pilot a service with a measurable customer cohort before financing a nationwide rollout. Stop initiatives that repeatedly miss their evidence gates without a credible remedy. Redirect scarce capital to propositions that demonstrate value. The purpose of review is not to humiliate managers. It is to improve the next decision while options remain affordable.

## Design incentives that agree with the strategy

A business may declare customer retention a strategic priority while rewarding sales teams only for new contracts. It may declare cash discipline while compensating managers on revenue booked. It may promise service quality while evaluating operations solely on cost cuts. In each case, the incentive system is the real strategy; the slide deck is an aspiration.

For every priority, identify the behaviours that make it likely and the measures that could unintentionally distort those behaviours. Pair speed with quality, volume with margin, and cost savings with customer outcomes. Assign one senior owner to resolve conflicts among targets rather than allowing each department to optimise its own dashboard.

An execution review should end in a decision. Continue the initiative, change scope, move resources, escalate a dependency or stop. Record the decision, the evidence used and when it will be revisited. Repeated meetings that produce no change in allocation or behaviour are a form of overhead, even when everyone leaves with an impressive action tracker.

## A twelve-week strategy-to-delivery reset

Weeks 1–2: list all initiatives, costs, owners and promised benefits. Cancel duplicates and identify those with no credible sponsor or demand. Weeks 3–4: choose the three to five priorities that matter most now, specify what will not be pursued and allocate decision rights. Weeks 5–8: establish delivery sprints, a shared risk register and one-page progress reports with outcome measures, not narrative theatre.

Weeks 9–12: review the evidence. Which customer metric changed? Which operating cost moved? Which assumptions failed? What has been learned at a price the business can afford? Use those answers to continue, redirect or terminate each initiative. Keep a record of decisions to prevent repeated arguments about matters the board already resolved.

A sound board pack should fit into a short dashboard: strategic objective, accountable owner, agreed milestone, current evidence, forecast benefit, funding at risk and decision required. Red status should be permissible when supported by facts. An organisation where every report is green until the programme collapses has not created discipline; it has created sophisticated surprise management.

Execution does not guarantee success. Competition changes, customers decline offers and external shocks occur. What disciplined execution provides is earlier detection, lower avoidable loss and a stronger capacity to adapt. It also gives leaders a way to be judged on results rather than adjectives.

## Boardroom takeaway

Ask every executive for three things: the strategic choice, the next proof point and the decision needed today. If the answer is another slogan, the strategy has not yet reached operations.
