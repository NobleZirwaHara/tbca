# Africa Was Given the Wrong Diagnosis. Now It Survives on Morphine.

*The case of privatisation, economic dependency and the urgent return to production*

Kruger Phiri | Founder & Managing Partner, The Business Clinic.Africa™ | 9 October 2026  

An economy cannot borrow its way into productivity. It cannot devalue its way into factories that do not exist. And it cannot sell its strategic assets, neglect the productive sectors, and then expect financial assistance to substitute for an industrial strategy.

Yet this is the uncomfortable question Africa must confront: did we spend decades treating the symptoms of economic weakness while leaving its productive foundations dangerously underdeveloped?

For many African countries, the late twentieth century brought a familiar policy prescription: liberalise markets, restructure or privatise state-owned enterprises, reduce fiscal imbalances and invite private capital to do what governments had struggled to do. The prescription was presented with confidence. Its results were far less uniform. \[1, 2\]

My argument is not that every public enterprise was efficient, that every privatisation failed, or that every international financial institution acted in bad faith. Those claims would not withstand scrutiny. The argument is sharper: when ownership reform becomes the objective, rather than a means to build productive capacity, a country can balance its books and still weaken its economic future.

## The diagnosis: sell first, produce later

Between roughly 1990 and 2005, privatisation became a prominent feature of reform across parts of Africa. In some countries, poorly run public enterprises had accumulated losses, drained public finances and delivered unreliable services. Reform was necessary. The question was what kind of reform, under what conditions, and in whose long-term interest. \[1, 2\]

Selling a badly managed company can improve efficiency where competition, investment and sound regulation follow. It can also transfer a public monopoly into private hands without solving the monopoly. A change in shareholder register is not the same thing as a change in national productive capacity.

A government may receive a once-off sale price and surrender a recurring income stream. Consumers may exchange one inefficient supplier for an expensive private supplier. Workers may lose employment before new industries are ready to absorb them. Equally, a well-structured sale can mobilise capital and raise service quality. These are possible outcomes, not universal verdicts.

The most important question should therefore never have been simply, 'How much can the state sell?' It should have been, 'What will this economy be able to produce, process and export ten years after the transaction?'

## The morphine: stabilisation without transformation

When a country faces a balance-of-payments crisis, external financing and macroeconomic stabilisation may be essential. No serious policymaker should dismiss their value. But emergency relief is not a permanent development model. A liquidity intervention may buy time. It does not, by itself, build irrigation networks, processing plants, transmission lines or export industries.

That is why I use the word 'morphine'. It describes an economic condition in which repeated financial treatment makes the immediate pain manageable while the underlying weakness remains unresolved. It is a criticism of dependency, not a claim that every IMF or World Bank programme caused Africa's difficulties.

The irony is difficult to ignore. Industrial policy has returned to prominence in advanced economies. An OECD handbook published in June 2026 identifies a renewed interest in deliberate government action to address strategic supply chains, weak productivity and industrial transition. \[3\] Africa should at least insist on the same right to design a credible production strategy, rather than debating only who owns yesterday's assets.

## Devaluation does not manufacture competitiveness

A weaker currency can make locally produced exports more competitive in foreign markets. That is the textbook case. But what happens when the country imports the fuel, machinery, fertiliser, spare parts and industrial inputs needed to produce those exports?

The cost of production rises. Households pay more for imported essentials. Firms find expansion more expensive. If inflation becomes persistent, the financing environment may tighten. A 2024 IMF study finds that depreciations in sub-Saharan Africa are associated with substantial increases in domestic inflation, with especially pronounced effects following large and persistent currency moves. \[4\]

A devaluation can help an economy that is ready to respond with output. It cannot create that capacity overnight. Exchange-rate policy should therefore support a productive strategy, not impersonate one.

## The real prescription: agriculture, mining and energy

Africa does not lack economic endowments. It lacks enough interconnected systems that turn those endowments into competitive, high-value products. UN Trade and Development has documented the continent's vulnerability to commodity dependence, infrastructure gaps and weak links between regional producers. Its remedy includes diversified value chains and stronger African markets. \[5\]

Start with agriculture. The objective cannot stop at growing crops. It must extend to irrigation, cold storage, processing, packaging, quality certification, logistics and supermarket-ready products. A tonne harvested is not the same as a tonne processed and sold profitably. The World Bank has also highlighted the employment and investment potential of better-performing African food systems. \[6\]

Then mining. A mineral exported in its raw state may earn foreign exchange, but it also leaves substantial opportunities for local services, beneficiation and manufacturing unexplored. Local processing must be assessed against energy costs, skills, scale, environmental standards and commercial demand. The point is not to process everything regardless of economics. It is to stop assuming that extraction alone is a complete industrial policy.

Finally, energy. Competitive production requires electricity that is dependable, affordable and available when factories need it. Energy investment should unlock industrial parks, mines, farms, cold chains and manufacturing clusters. Without reliable power, every other strategy becomes more expensive.

These three sectors are not isolated silos. Agriculture feeds processing. Mining creates demand for engineering and equipment. Energy enables both. Skills, transport, technology, financial services and effective institutions connect the system. That is how a resource economy begins to become a production economy.

## An uncomfortable proposition: public equity instead of perpetual taxation

Here is a proposition designed to provoke serious policy debate. For selected, transformative investments in agriculture, mining and energy, why should African governments not negotiate meaningful equity participation, potentially around 30%, in exchange for a clearly valued package of public contributions and investment incentives?

The more radical version would offer exemption from import duties on qualifying capital equipment, relief from eligible VAT costs and even a lifetime corporate-income-tax holiday in return for that public stake. The attraction is obvious: instead of relying only on annual tax receipts, the state could participate in the long-term value of a successful productive enterprise.

But a radical proposition is not the same as a sound, universal policy. A 30% stake has to be priced. Public land, mineral rights, infrastructure support or capital have measurable value; governments cannot assume that equity is free. A permanent tax holiday also gives up revenue even if an enterprise becomes extraordinarily profitable. IMF research published in 2025 warns that broad profit-tax exemptions often have limited investment benefits and significant fiscal costs. \[7\]

My preferred test is therefore rigorous rather than ideological: compare the lifetime public value of an equity-and-incentive arrangement against a conventional tax-and-royalty regime. Publish the assumptions. Independent experts should test the valuation. Make benefits conditional on verified capital expenditure, exports, local procurement, skills and operating milestones. Protect environmental and labour standards. Use sunset or review clauses where fiscal incentives are granted. And never confuse a politically negotiated shareholding with an investable commercial structure.

The 30% figure is a negotiating proposition, not a rule for every country or project. The principle is more important than the percentage: public participation should buy lasting productive capacity and measurable national value, not merely an announcement at an investment conference.

## Six decisions Africa's economic leaders must make

**1. Identify priority value chains.** Select products with demonstrable domestic or regional demand, credible export prospects and clear infrastructure requirements. Do not publish another catalogue of every sector imaginable.

**2. Measure production, not promises.** Track additional megawatts delivered, hectares made productive, tonnes processed locally, export contracts fulfilled and sustainable jobs created. Investment commitments are not investment outcomes.

**3. Structure public-private compacts.** Specify what the investor brings, what the state contributes, who bears risk, how public value is protected and what happens when targets are missed.

**4. Make industrial inputs competitive.** Fix electricity reliability, transport costs, customs delays, standards certification and access to patient capital before announcing expensive incentives.

**5. Build regional markets.** A national factory cannot always survive on a small domestic market. The World Bank's August 2026 report urges Africa to move beyond trade agreements on paper towards connected regional production hubs. \[8\]

**6. Publish a productivity scorecard.** Require ministers, development financiers and enterprise boards to report production and export outcomes each year. Stop celebrating the number of policy documents drafted.

## The question African leaders can no longer avoid

Privatisation is not a national development strategy. Neither is nationalisation. Neither is debt relief, currency adjustment or a tax holiday. Each can be useful, damaging or irrelevant depending on design and execution.

The organising question is more demanding: what is this nation capable of producing competitively, and what must government and business do together to increase that capability?

We should stop treating economic survival as evidence of economic health. We should stop mistaking a successful loan negotiation for an industrial breakthrough. And we should stop applauding raw-material exports without asking why more of the value is not created at home.

> Africa does not need another round of applause for the morphine. It needs an honest diagnosis, disciplined investment and a measurable return to production. The next generation will not judge us by how elegantly we managed scarcity. It will judge us by what we built.
