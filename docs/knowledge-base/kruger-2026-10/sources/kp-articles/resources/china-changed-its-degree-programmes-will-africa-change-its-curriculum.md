# China Changed Its Degree Programmes. Will Africa Change Its Curriculum?

*The 12,000-programme debate is not about burning certificates. It is about educating for the economies we intend to build.*

Kruger Phiri | Founder & Managing Partner, The Business Clinic.Africa™ | 7 October 2026  

Imagine a President commissioning a new university with marble entrances, a magnificent auditorium and a thousand graduation gowns. Five years later, ask a different question: how many graduates can commission a solar system, repair an irrigation pump, design a production line, analyse a mineral sample or turn a promising idea into a paying business? A university should produce informed citizens and new knowledge. It should also be able to demonstrate that its teaching serves real human and economic needs.

China has provided an uncomfortable reason for Africa to ask this question. But the headline circulating about “12,000 abolished degrees” needs to be corrected before we use it as an argument. An inaccurate statistic, however dramatic, is no foundation for education reform.

## What China actually changed

China’s Ministry of Education reported that, over roughly the twelve years from 2012, universities had added approximately 21,000 undergraduate programme locations and cancelled or suspended approximately 12,000. These are offerings of majors at individual institutions, not 12,000 distinct degree titles outlawed across the country. A programme can stop admitting students at one university and continue elsewhere. \[1\]

In the ministry’s results for the 2024 review, announced on 24 April 2025, institutions added 1,839 undergraduate programmes, cancelled 1,428 and suspended admissions to 2,220. The same update introduced 29 new types of majors, including carbon-neutrality science and engineering, medical device engineering and applications of artificial intelligence. These are official figures, but they do not establish that every discontinued offering was useless. \[2\]

The policy rationale is unmistakable: review programmes regularly against national strategy, labour-market signals, technological change, teaching quality and institutional capability. A further Chinese action plan for 2025–2027 calls for talent supply-and-demand matching, support for strategic and foundational disciplines, and warnings or reform for courses with weak demand, declining quality or inadequate facilities. \[3\]

Notice the final point. China is also protecting basic science and interdisciplinary learning. Its reform is not an instruction to abolish intellectual inquiry. The lesson for Africa is continuous curriculum review, not an indiscriminate bonfire of degrees.

## Which programmes were removed, and does Africa offer them?

Sichuan University’s July 2024 notice proposed withdrawing 31 undergraduate majors at that institution. Its list included broadcast and television studies, public-service management, e-commerce, environmental science, engineering cost management, metallurgy, information security, music, performance and applied physics. The university simultaneously proposed a new biomass technology and engineering major and other additions for consideration. These examples are specific to Sichuan University, not a list of 12,000 banned Chinese disciplines. \[4\]

African universities teach programmes with comparable names in business, public administration, media, environmental science, computing and engineering. But similarity of a degree title proves nothing about its quality or usefulness. A water-stressed African country may desperately need environmental scientists; a mineral-processing economy may need metallurgists whom one Chinese university has stopped recruiting into a particular course. Journalism and public administration remain essential to functioning democracies and institutions.

No verified, harmonised continental dataset was identified that establishes what proportion of African degree offerings matches China’s discontinued institutional programmes. Any claim that most African degrees are on a Chinese “scrap list” would therefore be speculation. What we can demand is a published country-by-country audit of enrolment, graduate outcomes, employer demand and curriculum content.

## A generation trained for vacancy notices, not production

Africa’s problem is not that every graduate chose the wrong course. It is that education policy and industrial policy too often travel on separate timetables. The World Bank stated in March 2026 that approximately 10–12 million young people enter Africa’s labour force annually, while only about 3 million formal jobs are created. These are broad continental estimates, not a measure of university unemployment alone. They expose how urgently enterprise formation, productivity and job creation must improve alongside education. \[5\]

The International Labour Organization’s 2024 youth employment report found that three in four young people in sub-Saharan Africa lacked secure work. It also reported widespread qualification-to-job mismatch among young adult workers in developing economies. The latter is not an Africa-only figure and should not be presented as one. Taken together, the findings make it impossible to defend a curriculum solely by pointing to examination pass rates or the number of degrees awarded. \[6\]

Yet it would be equally dishonest to blame the lecturer for the absence of electricity, investment, roads, capital or functioning firms. Even excellent graduates cannot fill jobs that the economy has not created. Reform must address both sides of the equation: the skills supplied and the productive demand for them.

## We already wrote the promise. Have we built the machinery?

Sustainable Development Goal 4.4 calls for relevant skills, including technical and vocational skills, for employment, decent jobs and entrepreneurship. SDG 8 addresses productive work and enterprise; SDG 9 addresses industrialisation and innovation. The African Union’s Agenda 2063 explicitly calls for a science- and technology-driven skills revolution, manufacturing, beneficiation and value addition. These ambitions are already documented. \[7, 8\]

The African Union’s Continental Education Strategy for Africa 2026–2035 and its Continental TVET Strategy 2025–2034 recognise the need to connect learning, work, innovation and lifelong skills development. The problem is not that Africa lacks a continental policy vocabulary. The problem is whether national budgets, examination systems, procurement choices and university incentives will now honour that vocabulary. \[9, 10\]

A country that names agro-processing as an industrial priority should identify the machine operators, microbiologists, food-safety inspectors, packaging designers, cold-chain technicians and sales professionals required to make it competitive. A country pursuing renewable power needs grid planners, electricians, protection engineers, welders and maintenance specialists. Training places should be traced back to investable projects and foreseeable demand, not merely to which department has always received a budget.

## Stop producing memory degrees. Start assessing competence.

An education system that rewards the reproduction of lecture notes but cannot verify practical performance is failing its students. Memorisation has a place: doctors, engineers, teachers and technicians need reliable knowledge. But factual recall cannot be the final test of competence. A graduate should be able to explain a principle, apply it safely, diagnose failure, work in a team and document the result.

Must every student write a thesis? No. Rigorous research is indispensable for some qualifications, particularly advanced and research-oriented degrees. For an applied programme, however, a validated prototype, audited farm-improvement plan, commissioned electrical installation, software product, clinical simulation, manufacturing process or community-service design may demonstrate the intended learning outcomes more convincingly than an untested dissertation. External examiners must still verify ethics, safety, originality and technical standards.

The choice is not thesis versus prototype. It is evidence versus ritual. A beautiful dissertation that never informs policy or practice can be a missed opportunity. A flashy prototype with no testing or scientific foundation can be a dangerous illusion. Universities must teach students when each kind of evidence is appropriate.

## The African curriculum I would put on the cabinet table

**1.** Power and infrastructure: Electrical installation, grid maintenance, mini-grid design, civil works, surveying, rail systems and industrial safety, assessed through supervised field tasks.

**2.** Agriculture and food industries: Irrigation, mechanisation, soil diagnostics, cold chains, processing, food safety, packaging and agribusiness accounting, taught with farms and processors.

**3.** Mining, materials and manufacturing: Geology, mineral processing, metallurgy, machine tooling, quality assurance, automation, repair and environmental rehabilitation, where commercially relevant.

**4.** Digital and AI-enabled production: Data literacy, cybersecurity, software engineering, process automation and responsible AI, linked to African problems and real users rather than tool demonstrations.

**5.** Health and human services: Nursing, laboratory science, biomedical equipment maintenance, public-health analytics and social-care delivery, with supervised clinical and community placements.

**6.** Trade and enterprise: Procurement, customs, logistics, export quality, entrepreneurship, finance and industrial management, assessed through operating budgets and actual customer requirements.

**7.** Civic and intellectual foundations: Languages, mathematics, history, ethics, governance, law, critical reasoning, arts and communication, integrated with technical learning instead of being dismissed as unnecessary.

These are proposed curriculum priorities, not an instruction that all 54 African countries teach identical programmes. An island economy, a landlocked agricultural exporter and a mature mining economy require different combinations and levels of specialisation. Every country should publish its reasons for selecting priority occupations and protect pathways for learners outside those priorities.

## Scholarships should purchase national capability, not simply certificates

A publicly funded scholarship should state the capability being developed. Which laboratory, municipality, enterprise, hospital, mine, processing facility or research programme needs it? Is the destination institution equipped to teach it? What industry exposure will the student receive? What support exists for returning graduates, or for knowledge transfer when graduates build careers elsewhere?

This is not a proposal to imprison scholarship recipients in predetermined jobs. It is a demand for public value and transparent selection. Scholarships in public policy, languages, law and education can be strategic too. The test is not whether a degree sounds technical. The test is whether the investment is justified and its contribution can be evaluated.

## Africa already has proof that change is possible

The World Bank reported in April 2025 that its Africa Higher Education Centers of Excellence initiative had supported more than 80 centres across 20 countries and enrolled over 90,000 students. It had also developed stronger industry links and advanced research across areas including agriculture, energy, health, mining and engineering. The figures concern that programme, not all African universities. They demonstrate that industry-linked, regionally collaborative education is already being practised on the continent. \[11\]

Governments should scale what works, evaluate it independently, and close or redesign programmes only after considering student rights, faculty capacity, evidence of demand and credible alternative pathways. Reform must be rigorous enough to change institutions and fair enough not to punish learners for yesterday’s policy choices.

## A twelve-month instruction to every African cabinet

**First 90 days:** Commission a national skills-and-curriculum audit. Publish programme-level admissions, completion, actual graduate outcomes where measurable, employer surveys, equipment adequacy and alignment to funded infrastructure and industrial projects. Protect personal data and distinguish absence of evidence from failure.

**By day 180:** Convene universities, TVET colleges, businesses, trade unions, professional councils, teachers and students to redesign priority qualifications. Publish practical competency standards, workplace-placement capacity and the full costs of laboratories, workshops and instructors.

**By day 270:** Reallocate an explicitly costed portion of new training funding toward reviewed priority programmes. Tie expansion to credible teaching capacity and supervised practical opportunities. Redesign weak programmes before considering closure; publish transition arrangements for affected students.

**By day 365:** Report the first dashboard publicly. Show which modules changed, how many learners completed assessed practical work, employers participating, placement quality, qualification recognition and actual programme cost. Then repeat the review annually.

The targets above are proposed governance milestones, not commitments already made by any African government. The cabinet should disclose where data are unavailable rather than manufacture success. No President should claim an employment outcome merely because a training certificate was issued.

## A direct question to Africa’s Presidents

Your country probably has an education strategy, an industrialisation strategy, an investment strategy and a youth employment strategy. Can you demonstrate that all four are buying the same future? If not, which one is being implemented, and which three are decorating official shelves?

Stop announcing university openings without specifying the capabilities they will build. Stop treating every scholarship as a diplomatic trophy. Stop confusing a graduate’s ability to recite with an economy’s ability to produce. And stop importing ready-made curricula without checking whether they answer African priorities.

> China’s 12,000 programme changes are not a template for mass cancellation. They are a reminder that education must be reviewed, challenged and renewed. Africa should do something more ambitious than copy China’s list: build its own evidence-led curriculum, invest in the equipment and teachers to deliver it, and test graduates on what they understand, create and improve. The most uncomfortable examination is now for the Presidents, ministers and university councils themselves. What, exactly, are we preparing the next generation to build?
