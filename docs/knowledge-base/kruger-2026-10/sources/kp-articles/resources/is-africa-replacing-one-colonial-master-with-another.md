# Is Africa Replacing One Colonial Master With Another?

*The contest between the dollar, the yuan, BRICS and African financial sovereignty*

Kruger Phiri | Founder & Managing Partner, The Business Clinic.Africa™  
KP Thought Leadership | Proposed publication date: 20 October 2026  

## 1. The danger is not who holds the leash. It is why Africa still wears one.

Africa is witnessing an extraordinary rearrangement of global influence. The United States remains the centre of an unmatched financial network. China is building alternative trade and payment channels. BRICS is negotiating greater settlement choice. African institutions are creating their own financial infrastructure. Meanwhile, wars, sanctions, tariffs and blocked shipping lanes remind the world that the freedom to sell a commodity is not the same as the freedom to receive payment for it.

An uncomfortable question follows. If an African government replaces a dollar loan with a renminbi loan, a Western-built railway with a Chinese-built railway, or a European correspondent bank with another foreign-owned settlement corridor, has it gained sovereignty? Or has it simply negotiated a new address for the dependency?

The question is not an accusation against China, the United States, Europe or any other partner. Trade and finance are not inherently colonial. Countries may benefit enormously from foreign markets, credit, technology and investment. The danger begins when one party can dictate essential choices because the other has no credible alternative. Economic independence is not isolation. It is the ability to choose, negotiate, produce, refuse and still function.

A government that cannot pay for critical imports without the approval of outside financial institutions, cannot manufacture the goods it imports, and cannot finance its productive enterprises on sustainable terms is not strategically free. But a government that denounces external influence while running an insolvent utility, a dysfunctional port and an opaque procurement system is not strategically free either. Sovereignty must be measured in capabilities, not slogans.

## 2. The dollar's power is real, but its reach is frequently misdescribed.

The United States dollar remains the most important currency in international finance. The Bank for International Settlements reported that it was on one side of 89.2% of worldwide foreign-exchange trades in April 2025. The International Monetary Fund's figures published on 30 September 2026 put the dollar at 56.70% of reported official foreign-exchange reserves in the second quarter of 2026. \[1\]\[2\]

These are different measurements. The first measures the currency used in foreign-exchange transactions, where both sides of a pair are counted. The second measures the currency composition of official reserve assets. Neither says that 89% of world trade is invoiced in dollars. Yet both demonstrate how profoundly the dollar is embedded in global balance sheets, market liquidity, reserves and risk management.

There are reasons for that strength beyond political pressure: deep US Treasury markets, liquidity, enforceable contracts, financial-market scale and network effects. Importers, exporters, lenders and central banks use dollars partly because others use dollars. Dollar liquidity can lower financing costs, and abandoning it without an alternative can raise them. Any serious African strategy must acknowledge these advantages instead of pretending they are propaganda.

But a currency is not merely a printed note. A cross-border payment depends on banks, messaging standards, clearing institutions, correspondent accounts, liquidity providers, screening systems and settlement rules. A supplier may invoice in dollars even when neither party is American. If the transaction passes through institutions subject to US law, US restrictions may matter. Banks outside the United States may also limit dealings to manage sanctions, regulatory and reputational exposure. None of this means that Washington has an unrestricted legal power to freeze every dollar held anywhere on earth. Jurisdiction, ownership, sanctions design and the relevant institution matter.

SWIFT is primarily a financial messaging network, not itself a currency or the account that holds the money. CIPS, central-bank settlement systems and correspondent banking perform related but distinct roles. Treating every institution in this chain as an American-controlled dollar account obscures the operational exposure that policymakers actually need to manage.

The modern question is therefore not simply, 'Whose currency?' It is: who operates the infrastructure, writes the rules, supplies the liquidity, decides access, bears the risk and can deny the service?

## 3. Sanctions show what financial dependence can mean in practice.

The word 'freeze' must be used carefully. The US Office of Foreign Assets Control explains that blocked property cannot ordinarily be transferred or dealt with, although legal title usually remains with the blocked owner. Blocking is not automatically the same thing as confiscation. \[3\]

Following Russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the multinational REPO task force announced that approximately US\$300 billion in Russian central-bank assets had been immobilised. That was a coordinated international action, not a unilateral demonstration that the US controls every foreign reserve. \[4\] US restrictions on Iranian financial institutions likewise show how access to American persons, property and financial relationships can be constrained. \[5\]

Sanctions have a legitimate policy rationale in some circumstances: governments use them to respond to aggression, human-rights abuses, terrorism financing and other threats without deploying troops. Critics argue that their broader effects can be severe, especially when over-compliance impedes lawful humanitarian trade and ordinary commercial activity. Both propositions deserve scrutiny. The point for Africa is less about endorsing or rejecting particular sanctions than understanding exposure before a crisis arrives.

Three practical questions should appear in every sovereign treasury risk assessment. How much of the country's usable liquidity sits with institutions whose access may be restricted? Which essential imports depend on a single currency or correspondent-bank corridor? What legal, operational and reserve arrangements allow legitimate trade to continue if one corridor fails?

Alternative payment rails are not a licence to evade lawful sanctions. They are a reason to diversify lawful trading relationships, improve compliance systems and reduce single-point failures. Financial sovereignty built on illegal or opaque transactions is a fragile substitute for dependence.

## 4. The Iran war and the Strait of Hormuz are a warning, not proof that the petrodollar has died.

The 2026 war involving Iran and the United States and Israel disrupted the Strait of Hormuz and drew attention to reports that some vessels were asked to pay transit charges in Chinese yuan. Reporting in April described a de facto Iranian toll regime, while also noting uncertainty about the scale of payments and the legality of imposed transit charges. \[6\]\[7\] By October, shipping attacks and disrupted flows continued to raise the commercial stakes. \[8\]

The distinction is fundamental. A reported transit fee denominated in yuan is not the same as the world's oil contracts suddenly moving into yuan. Nor is it evidence that Chinese financial infrastructure is now beyond geopolitical reach. Insurance, freight, oil prices, bank guarantees and the final settlement currency are separate parts of a complex transaction.

The so-called petrodollar is best understood as the established practice of pricing and settling much global oil trade in dollars, supported by liquidity and financial-market depth. It is not a single switch that any government can turn off overnight. The Hormuz conflict demonstrates that coercive power can operate through shipping lanes as well as bank accounts. Replacing one chokepoint with another does not solve Africa's problem.

For an African oil importer, the urgent questions are operational. Are there alternative suppliers? Are strategic stocks adequate? Can the importer hedge price and currency risk? Is energy demand being reduced through reliable domestic generation and efficiency? An African country will not negotiate from strength because a tanker pays a toll in yuan. It will negotiate from strength when an interruption no longer brings its economy to a halt.

## 5. Five dependencies that a new payment currency cannot repair.

First is productive dependence. An economy exports raw copper, cocoa or crude oil and then imports finished wire, chocolate or fuel. The payment system may change while the distribution of industrial margins remains intact.

Second is financial dependence. Enterprises borrow abroad because local capital markets are shallow, guarantees are scarce and project preparation is weak. The borrower then carries foreign-exchange liabilities against revenues earned in a weaker local currency. Relabelling a dollar debt as yuan debt does not remove this mismatch.

Third is technological dependence. Banks and factories may depend on foreign software, payment gateways, chips, platforms, cloud services and proprietary maintenance. Payments can become faster while bargaining power over technology becomes weaker.

Fourth is commercial dependence. An exporter with a single buyer, a farmer with a single commodity trader and a state-owned enterprise with one equipment supplier may have limited room to bargain regardless of invoice currency.

Fifth is institutional dependence. Weak tax administration, unreliable statistics, poorly governed state enterprises and secretive contracts compel governments to accept costly financing terms. Some external constraints are real. Others reflect domestic failures that no foreign power imposed.

The economic test is simple: after the transaction, which country owns the knowledge, controls the asset, captures the value added and can compete without political protection?

## 6. China's payment infrastructure is an important option. It is not an African payment system.

China's Cross-Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS) supports renminbi cross-border payment and settlement. Standard Bank became the first bank on the continent authorised to offer CIPS transactions in 2025. In June 2026, Standard Bank and the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China announced authorisation to establish a jointly operated Renminbi Clearing Bank of Africa, with capacity across 19 African countries. \[9\]\[10\]

By 27 July 2026, Standard Bank reported that it had processed more than CNY 8 billion, approximately US\$1.2 billion at the conversion used in its announcement, through CIPS. Its expanding client access covered Angola, Ghana, Kenya, Lesotho and Tanzania as well as South Africa. \[11\] China's CIPS operator reported CNY 162.3 trillion in aggregate global transaction value across January to September 2026. That last figure is a global system statistic and must not be misrepresented as African transaction volume. \[12\]

These are meaningful developments. African firms purchasing Chinese equipment can potentially reduce unnecessary currency conversions, improve settlement efficiency and access renminbi liquidity. A financing transaction in yuan can also align repayment with yuan-denominated receipts or procurement, where the underlying economics justify it.

But the bank remains a commercial intermediary; CIPS is Chinese payment infrastructure; and the renminbi is governed by Chinese monetary, legal and financial arrangements. The fact that the transfer avoids one intermediary currency does not place Africa in control of China's monetary policy or banking rules. An alternative is valuable precisely because it creates choice. Dependence reappears if that alternative becomes the only choice.

The continent should welcome CIPS, while demanding equivalent investment in African-owned rails, African market-making capacity, interoperability and regulatory competence. It should also ask for reciprocal access to Chinese markets, technology transfer and productive investment rather than celebrating the payment route in isolation.

## 7. The sharper China question is not what currency Africa pays in. It is what Africa sells.

Trade data make the industrial issue impossible to avoid. Associated Press reported that China-Africa trade reached approximately US\$348 billion in 2025, including around US\$225 billion in Chinese exports to Africa and US\$123 billion in imports from Africa. That implies an African bilateral goods deficit of approximately US\$102 billion: 225 minus 123. \[13\]

Those figures cover reported merchandise trade and must not be confused with all services, investment income or the continent's total balance of payments. A bilateral deficit does not by itself prove exploitation. Investment equipment imported today may support higher local output tomorrow; country-level and sector-level effects differ. But a persistent pattern in which raw materials leave and finished products return demands a clear industrial response.

A Boston University and African Economic Research Consortium study likewise found that Africa's exports to China remained heavily concentrated in extractive products. Its 2026 bulletin reported that some Chinese development-credit flows have turned negative on a net-transfer basis as debt repayments exceeded new disbursements in recent years. \[14\]

There is no serious case for shunning China. There is a serious case for negotiating better. Why must an African government borrow to buy an entire imported factory rather than require local supplier development? Why approve minerals exports without credible processing and power plans? Why enter infrastructure contracts without publishing repayment schedules, procurement assumptions and local-content outcomes?

The answer is not to impose uneconomic local-content rules in every case. It is to assess projects rigorously and build competitive African firms into supply chains. A government should judge a foreign-financed industrial park by export earnings, skills transferred, local procurement, paid taxes and jobs sustained, not by the nationality of the opening ceremony's guest of honour.

## 8. PAPSS offers something strategically different: African capacity.

The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS), developed by Afreximbank with the African Union and AfCFTA institutions, enables participating banks and payment providers to execute cross-border payments in local currencies. Its published model includes instant payment instructions, pre-funding and net settlement; it describes a near-instant processing target of 120 seconds. \[15\]\[16\]

The important nuance is in the settlement design. PAPSS permits customers to pay and receive in their respective local currencies, but its published net-settlement process also uses hard-currency settlement accounts at Afreximbank for the resulting net positions. It reduces the need for every underlying African transaction to make a separate, cumbersome foreign-currency round trip. It does not make the need for reserve liquidity disappear. \[15\]

Its reach has grown. Afreximbank said in February 2026 that Kenya's Pesalink integration connected more than 80 Pesalink participants with over 160 PAPSS-participating banks. In July 2026, the Bank of Central African States joined PAPSS, connecting the six CEMAC member states to the broader network. These figures describe specific participation arrangements, not a claim that every African person or bank can already send every payment instantaneously. \[17\]\[18\]

Imagine a Kenyan manufacturer selling machinery parts to a Ghanaian distributor. Previously, the parties might route settlement through correspondent banks and convert twice, even though neither operates in the United States. With a compliant participating route, the customer can pay in the supported local currency while the supplier receives the supported destination currency. The banks still manage rates, liquidity and settlement risk. But the client experience becomes simpler and African institutions retain a larger role.

That is not a declaration of currency war against Washington or Beijing. It is the construction of a public-interest market utility. The prize is a continental trading network in which African businesses can buy from African businesses without treating a third currency as the indispensable intermediary for every invoice.

## 9. BRICS is diversifying financial diplomacy, not issuing a common money tomorrow.

The September 2026 BRICS New Delhi Declaration acknowledged work by the BRICS Payment Task Force on interoperability, cross-border payment mechanisms and local-currency trade settlement. It recognised that countries have different priorities and that there is no single model appropriate to all. \[19\]

The often-repeated statement that a BRICS currency is about to replace the dollar goes beyond the official position. At the September 2026 summit, India's foreign ministry publicly stated that no common BRICS currency proposal was then under consideration. That does not preclude future ideas; it means an operative shared currency should not be represented as a present fact. \[20\]

BRICS is also not the G20, and neither is synonymous with the G7. South Africa is a member of BRICS and the G20. The African Union became a permanent G20 member in 2023. An African country need not abandon existing international forums to diversify its trade relationships. Multiple seats can create leverage, provided African negotiators arrive with common priorities and technical preparation. \[21\]

A smart African strategy is neither 'anti-West' nor 'pro-East'. It is pro-African competitiveness and accountable reciprocity. Engagement with BRICS should be judged by industrial projects funded, market access gained, development finance priced fairly and intellectual property developed in Africa. Diplomatic photographs are not a balance-sheet asset.

## 10. Cryptocurrency is not a shortcut to sovereignty.

African banks are moving into digital assets, but the meaning of their announcements must be kept precise. Absa announced a partnership with Ripple in October 2025 to provide institutional digital-asset custody infrastructure in South Africa. FNB advertises cryptocurrency investment access through its app. Safekeeping a client's tokens and enabling investment are not the same thing as operating a sovereign cross-border payments network. \[22\]\[23\]

South Africa's central bank states that crypto assets are not legal tender; its regulatory framework has developed through financial-services licensing and anti-money-laundering requirements. Crypto assets can be volatile, vulnerable to cybercrime, and exposed to failures of custodians or issuers. Some stablecoins are linked to the US dollar and may import the very dollar exposure their advocates claim to remove. \[24\]

Three things must be distinguished. Bitcoin is a volatile digital asset with a decentralised network. A stablecoin is typically a privately issued token designed to track another asset, often a currency, and is only as sound as its reserves, redemption terms, governance and relevant law. A central-bank digital currency is a potential form of public money, dependent on its central bank's design and legislation. Tokenised deposits and bank-led payment systems are different again.

Technology can lower friction. It cannot abolish insolvency, fraud, exchange-rate exposure or the rule of law. Governments should regulate digital-asset custody and payments proportionately, preserve consumer protection, and test interoperability and cyber resilience before declaring a digital token the new foundation of national commerce.

## 11. The foreign-exchange arithmetic politicians rarely put on a stage.

Consider a South African importer buying machinery worth US\$1 million. At an assumed exchange rate of R18 per dollar, the invoice costs R18 million. If the rate weakens to R20 before payment, the rand cost becomes R20 million. That is an additional R2 million, or 11.1% of the original rand cost, even if the dollar supplier has not changed its price.

Now suppose the same machinery is priced in yuan instead. The importer may avoid converting rand into dollars and then dollars into yuan. That can reduce conversion costs where the bank offers competitive direct liquidity. But the rand can still weaken against the yuan. The currency mismatch survives unless revenues, liabilities or hedges are aligned. These figures are a hypothetical demonstration, not observed South African exchange rates on a specific transaction date.

For an exporter, the opposite movement can increase local-currency earnings but raise the cost of imported equipment and foreign debt. A finance minister should not declare victory after replacing dollar liabilities with yuan liabilities while leaving the same unhedged operating exposure. A treasury should compare all-in interest cost, fees, swap prices, convertibility, maturities, governing law and concentration risk before choosing the currency.

There is also a distinction between transaction costs and national economic savings. A 1% reduction in conversion and transfer charges on an eligible US\$100 million flow would amount to US\$1 million in gross annual fee savings if every transaction received that reduction. It would not automatically increase national GDP by US\$1 million, nor account for system implementation costs, spreads or liquidity management. Meaningful policy starts by measuring actual corridor costs, not announcing hypothetical continental windfalls.

## 12. A six-question test for a new colonial dependency.

Calling every difficult contract 'colonialism' weakens analysis. The better method is to examine bargaining power. First: ownership. Who owns the physical asset, customer data, patents, shares and trading rights after the financing is repaid? Second: revenue capture. Which businesses receive the processing margins, maintenance revenue, interest and export earnings?

Third: choice. Can an African borrower, importer or government switch suppliers, currencies or lenders at reasonable cost? Fourth: risk allocation. Who carries construction delay, commodity-price swings, depreciation and sovereign guarantees? Fifth: institutional voice. Can African regulators and courts meaningfully oversee the arrangement and resolve disputes? Sixth: development spillovers. Does the contract generate durable local skills, research capability, suppliers and taxable activity?

Apply these six questions to a French, American, Indian, Turkish, Gulf, Chinese or African investor. The nationality changes nothing about the test. A foreign investor that builds a viable factory, trains engineers and accepts balanced risk sharing may contribute more to economic sovereignty than a politically celebrated domestic monopoly that produces nothing.

Real independence is not hostility to foreigners. It is freedom from arrangements that African institutions cannot evaluate, contest or change.

## 13. The missing instrument is often not a new currency. It is a bankable African project.

Africa has payment-system ambitions, but its investment problem begins much earlier than the final settlement instruction. An investor needs permits, reliable power, credible offtake, accurate land records, enforceable contracts, audited project costs, resilient logistics and technically competent sponsors. Without those conditions, a cheaper payments corridor delivers little.

Afreximbank estimated in its 2025 African Trade Report that Africa's trade finance gap was around US\$100 billion and that intra-African trade reached US\$220.3 billion in 2024, representing 14.4% of Africa's total trade. These are different indicators, each with its own year and definition. \[25\]

Closing the investment gap should begin with transaction-ready pipelines: agro-processing, pharmaceuticals, transport equipment, power components, mineral beneficiation and regional logistics. For each project, governments should publish a credible demand assessment, tariff and currency assumptions, procurement plan, environmental safeguards, offtake commitments, downside case and repayment structure.

Concessional finance, development-bank participation and appropriately capped public guarantees can then mobilise capital where public value is demonstrable. Resource-backed commitments need particular care: they should disclose collateral, avoid secret commodity pledges and prevent future generations from losing strategic assets through today's poorly priced borrowing. The objective is bankability without surrendering public accountability.

## 14. Build African institutions that no foreign country can shut down at will.

An African treasury cannot repeal foreign sanctions law or make every trade partner accept its currency. It can, however, improve the systems within its control. Governments and central banks should treat payments resilience as critical infrastructure. That includes domestic real-time payment rails; interoperable central-bank settlement; redundancy in cross-border messaging; strong bank supervision; crisis liquidity facilities; and coordinated cyber-security standards.

Competition is essential. PAPSS should be assessed and improved on actual adoption, costs, reliability and settlement risk. CIPS and other foreign channels should remain available where commercially useful and legally appropriate. Banks should publish comparable all-in transfer costs and time-to-credit figures so customers can decide based on evidence, not geopolitical branding.

Countries must also deepen local-currency bond markets and longer-term domestic savings pools. An African industrialist who can obtain twenty-year capital at a sustainable fixed local-currency rate has gained a freedom no currency slogan can provide. Equally, an African manufacturer that earns export revenue across several markets is less vulnerable to the sudden loss of a single foreign buyer.

## 15. A practical programme for African presidents: 100 days, one year, three years.

**Within 100 days:** Direct the finance ministry and central bank to publish a confidential, independently verified map of foreign-currency funding, reserve custodians, correspondent-bank dependencies, critical-import payment routes and exposure to sanctions and technology concentration. Release a public summary that does not compromise national security. Establish a cross-ministerial payment resilience and industrial-finance task force including private banks, exporters, manufacturers and competition authorities.

**Within one year:** Benchmark the ten largest trade corridors for end-to-end cost, rejection rates and settlement delays. Pilot at least three commercially justified PAPSS corridors where participation and domestic regulation permit. Negotiate transparent renminbi settlement facilities for eligible China-related trade without mandating exclusive use. Implement clear rules for digital-asset custody and operational risk. Prepare twenty independently assessed, finance-ready industrial projects, each with a costed local-supplier development plan. Publish all material sovereign guarantee commitments and major resource-linked borrowing exposures.

**Within three years:** Expand settlement interoperability across regional economic communities. Support pooled trade-finance and guarantee facilities with disclosed capital adequacy and loss limits. Build specialised currency-hedging and market-making capacity. Link industrial parks to power reliability, ports, rail and verifiable export contracts. Create public procurement scorecards showing whether foreign-financed projects develop African suppliers and skills. Require annual external evaluation and close programmes that do not meet their published benchmarks.

No president can achieve this alone. Central banks, regulators, private businesses, universities, pension funds, Afreximbank, the African Union and the AfCFTA institutions all have distinct responsibilities. Coordination is preferable to a grand announcement about a currency that does not yet exist.

## 16. Eight measurements that distinguish independence from theatre.

A serious national dashboard should disclose: (1) the share of eligible intra-African payments completed in supported local currencies; (2) median end-to-end cross-border payment fees, including foreign-exchange spreads; (3) payment time and failure rates by corridor; (4) trade-finance approval rates for viable small and medium enterprises; (5) the share of export earnings generated by processed and manufactured goods; (6) the share of foreign-currency sovereign and corporate obligations naturally hedged or appropriately risk-managed; (7) local procurement and employment achieved under major foreign-financed industrial projects; and (8) concentration of reserves, key software providers, correspondent banks and major export buyers.

Each measure requires a baseline, a reporting owner, a methodology and an independent audit. Without that discipline, governments will simply replace one ceremonial language of dependence with another. The scoreboard should show whether an African enterprise can make a product, find a customer, receive payment, invest its profits and expand across borders on terms it understands.

The strategic outcome is not to make the dollar disappear. It is to ensure that African production and lawful trade do not collapse merely because a particular currency, bank, supplier or foreign capital withdraws.

## 17. Who wins when Africa changes financial masters?

The United States benefits when global trade and reserves rely on its liquid financial markets and legal infrastructure. China benefits when renminbi trade, Chinese financial services and demand for Chinese goods deepen. International banks earn revenues from financing, custody, exchange, settlement and risk management. African banks also benefit when they lead new services. None of these commercial interests is inherently improper.

The central question is whether African households, enterprises and public finances obtain durable returns. Faster payment for a container of imported manufactured goods is useful. More useful is an African enterprise producing the goods that fill the container. A new clearing bank is valuable. More valuable is an industrial customer with competitive exports and multiple financing choices. A new currency agreement may be prudent. It is not a substitute for competent government.

Africa should trade with the West, China, India, the Gulf and whoever offers fair, legal, competitive terms. It should welcome American market access, Chinese manufacturing expertise, European technology, Gulf capital, Indian pharmaceuticals and African innovation where the transactions are beneficial and transparent. Its leaders should refuse the false choice between permanent dependence on one bloc and compulsory allegiance to another.

A country ceases to be a captive not when it changes the nationality of its lender, but when it becomes capable of negotiating a better contract, declining a bad one and building productive alternatives. That is a harder political project than announcing a new alliance. It requires functioning electricity, disciplined budgets, predictable courts, skilled workers, export-oriented firms and honest data.

Africa's presidents should answer a final question without ceremony: after the next decade of diversification, will the continent own more enterprises, process more of its resources, finance more investment domestically and trade more with itself? Or will it merely have learnt to pronounce 'dependency' in a different currency?

**Do not change the master and call it freedom. Change the productive capacity, the institutions and the terms of trade. Then no master is required.**
