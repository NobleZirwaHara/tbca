# Africa Is Not Poor. It Is Under-Invested.

*The continent has supplied the raw materials. It is time to build the industries.*

Kruger Phiri | Founder & Managing Partner, The Business Clinic.Africa™ | 16 September 2026  

A continent can be rich in resources and still have millions of poor citizens. That is not a contradiction. It is a warning about how its economy is organised. Africa’s central problem is not an absence of economic assets. It is the persistent failure to invest enough in the systems that turn those assets into productive industries, skilled employment and shared prosperity.

We have been taught to celebrate the discovery of a mineral deposit as if discovery were development. A mining licence is announced. An export terminal opens. A ship sails. Then we announce another investment success. But what was manufactured? Which African supplier won a contract? How many engineers were trained? Which factory will still operate when the deposit runs out?

A serious country should answer those questions before it congratulates itself.

## The numbers expose the contradiction

The International Energy Agency reports that Africa supplies around 75% of global manganese, 70% of cobalt and nearly 20% of copper, yet captures less than 1% of the value generated by manufacturing clean-energy technologies and their components. The first figures measure raw-material supply; the last measures downstream technology manufacturing. They are different stages of a value chain, which is precisely the point. \[1\]

The United States Geological Survey estimated that the Democratic Republic of the Congo mined about 226,000 metric tonnes of contained cobalt in 2024. South Africa produced approximately 19.6 million tonnes of manganese ore in that year. These figures concern different commodities and different measurement units; they should not be added together. \[2, 3\]

Agriculture provides an equally difficult comparison. The International Cocoa Organization’s 2024/25 annual report projected African cocoa production at 3.253 million tonnes, or 69.2% of global supply. Separately, FAO data published through Our World in Data show African cassava production of approximately 223 million tonnes in 2024. The opportunity is not to pretend that all these tonnes can be exported or processed profitably. It is to ask why local productivity, storage, processing, packaging and distribution do not command a larger share of the business. \[4, 5\]

Now consider the factory floor. The African Development Bank’s Africa Industrialisation Index 2025 reports US\$351 billion in African manufacturing value added in 2025, but less than 2% of global manufacturing output and only 1.4% of global manufacturing exports. Manufacturing exists and is growing, yet remains too small relative to the continent’s resources and ambitions. \[6\]

These are not statistics to weaponise against every investor. They are evidence that the location of value creation deserves at least as much scrutiny as the volume of raw exports.

## We designed too much of the economy around the exit gate

Across many value chains, the dominant sequence has been familiar: extract or harvest locally, ship material abroad, undertake higher-value transformation elsewhere, and import finished products or equipment. Colonial economic structures helped establish that pattern; post-independence policy failures, weak infrastructure, governance problems, financial constraints and the decisions of private businesses have helped preserve parts of it.

The result is not that all African exports are raw or that beneficiation is always more profitable. Morocco, South Africa and other countries already manufacture competitively in selected industries. Côte d’Ivoire processes substantial cocoa volumes. There are successes. The problem is that such productive depth is not yet the organising principle of enough national investment strategies.

UN Trade and Development identifies dependence on commodities, weak logistics and limited regional linkages as persistent vulnerabilities. Using its commodity-dependence threshold of more than 60% of merchandise export earnings from primary commodities, UNCTAD classified 45 of 54 African countries as commodity-dependent in its earlier assessment. The precise composition changes over time, but the structural concern is clear. \[7, 8\]

An economy that imports machinery can still grow. An economy that attracts foreign capital can become wealthier. The error is not trading with the world. The error is treating the export of unprocessed output as the end of industrial development rather than the beginning of a more demanding commercial project.

## Fifty years of extraction: ask the right accounting question

How much oil, gas, gold, copper, cobalt, platinum, manganese, bauxite, chromium and phosphate has Africa produced and exported over the past 50 years? How much cocoa, coffee, cashew, cotton, tea, cassava, palm oil, sugarcane, maize and sesame has it harvested and traded? These 20 supply chains merit a public accounting exercise. They are a proposed audit set, not a verified ranking of the continent’s top 20 exports.

I will not place a fabricated trillion-dollar total beneath that question. Production is not the same as export volume. Gross export receipts are not the same as profit, retained national income or lost wealth. Prices, grades, reporting coverage, inflation and the domestic use of products have changed materially between 1976 and 2025. Even a legitimate export series would require careful separation of raw materials, intermediate goods and finished products to avoid double counting.

Instead, every African treasury, statistics office and natural-resource ministry should publish a reconciled fifty-year value-chain ledger: annual tonnes or barrels produced; quantities exported; recorded export values; the domestic-processing share; public royalties and taxes received; and the share of supplier contracts awarded to local firms. FAOSTAT, USGS, OPEC, UN Comtrade and national customs and company disclosures offer building blocks, not a ready-made audited continental total. \[2, 9, 10, 11\]

Without that ledger, governments can celebrate export growth without being able to explain what the country retained. Economic sovereignty requires better accounting as well as better contracts.

## The investment model must change, not merely the investor

The test for a new investment should move beyond how many hectares, barrels or tonnes it controls. It must include the facilities, capabilities and markets it creates. A new copper operation should be evaluated for commercially viable smelting, refining, electrical-products and supplier opportunities. A cocoa programme should be evaluated for bean quality, grinding, ingredients, packaging, branding and market access. An agricultural corridor should be measured by cold-chain reliability and products sold, not hectares announced.

This does not mean forcing every mine to build a refinery or banning raw exports overnight. Some forms of processing require electricity, technology, scale, water or markets that a given country does not yet possess. Industrial policy fails when governments confuse aspiration with a bankable project. Regional specialisation, joint ventures, predictable rules and competitive procurement can create value more sustainably than blanket mandates.

Electricity is an obvious starting point. According to the IEA, roughly 600 million people in sub-Saharan Africa were without electricity in 2024. This is a human-development crisis and an industrial cost. A factory without dependable power is not a credible competitor, whatever tax incentives it receives. \[12\]

The World Bank Group and African Development Bank’s Mission 300 seeks to provide first-time electricity access to 300 million Africans by 2030. It is a programme target, not a claim that those connections have already been delivered. African industrial corridors should connect generation and grids to productive loads, while appropriately chosen mini-grids and standalone systems serve communities beyond the grid. \[13\]

## What would a different US\$100 billion investment decision buy?

Consider an illustrative, not forecast, five-year investment envelope of US\$100 billion. Direct US\$40 billion toward commercially appraised factories and processing facilities; US\$35 billion toward power, rail, industrial roads, ports and water; US\$15 billion toward supplier credit and working capital; and US\$10 billion toward skills, testing laboratories, industrial technology and project preparation. The allocations are a policy illustration, not an announced funding commitment or an estimate of Africa’s total financing requirement.

Suppose, purely to show the scale of the decision, that the average factory investment were US\$40 million and each completed facility directly employed 150 people. US\$40 billion divided by US\$40 million equals 1,000 illustrative facilities; multiplied by 150 equals 150,000 potential direct jobs. Those assumed costs and staffing ratios are not empirical forecasts. Heavy refineries, food-processing lines and automated component plants have very different capital and employment profiles. Indirect jobs, displacement, capacity utilisation, export receipts and project viability cannot be calculated responsibly from these assumptions alone.

The point of that arithmetic is discipline. A President who promises one million factory jobs must be able to show the projects, capital intensity, available electricity, suppliers, output contracts and credible labour coefficients behind the promise. Otherwise, the promise is not an industrial plan. It is advertising.

Independent modelling is available for some wider reforms. The World Bank estimated in a 2022 scenario that deeper implementation of the African Continental Free Trade Area, alongside stronger investment and related reforms, could raise continental income by 9%, or US\$571 billion, relative to a 2035 baseline, and support 18 million additional jobs. These are modelled, conditional outcomes, not observed results and not the expected return on the hypothetical US\$100 billion above. \[14\]

## Every President should sign a five-year industrial contract

**1. Select three competitive value chains.** Choose them using demand, energy cost, comparative advantage, environmental limits and export feasibility. Publish why they won and why others did not.

**2. Fund productive infrastructure first.** Match substations, industrial water, roads, rail sidings and customs upgrades to signed industrial projects rather than politically selected sites.

**3. Negotiate investment for local capability.** Require transparent supplier-development and skills plans, with attainable milestones and consequences for non-delivery. Do not promise permanent fiscal concessions for photographs and speeches.

**4. Build processing and manufacturing in regional clusters.** Use the African Continental Free Trade Area to support production at viable scale. Not every country needs an identical plant; every country needs a commercially defensible role in a regional supply chain.

**5. Make African enterprise ownership investable.** Offer long-term finance, payment certainty, quality certification, research partnerships and access to public procurement under fair and transparent rules.

**6. Publish the scorecard annually.** Track operating factories, locally purchased inputs, reliable electricity supplied, export revenue by processing stage, productive employment, unit costs and returns to public support. Report failures with the same prominence as openings.

The scorecard must show trade balances and import substitution without celebrating protection for its own sake. An expensive domestic product that cannot compete may simply transfer costs to African households. Measure the real foreign-exchange saving after imported inputs and the quality-adjusted price paid by consumers.

## A question for the leaders who still prefer ceremonies to production

Africa is not a single economy, and no single industrial prescription fits 54 countries. But one question should confront every cabinet: after five years in office, is your country better at turning its agricultural, mineral and human resources into competitive products that people will pay for?

If the answer is a catalogue of conferences, concessions, export permits and loan agreements, the investment model has not changed. It has acquired new stationery.

> A deposit beneath the ground is potential. A harvested crop is an opportunity. Wealth is created when people, capital, institutions and technology turn those assets into reliable output. Africa does not need to be congratulated for what nature placed on the continent. It should be judged by what its leaders and businesses build from it.
