# Opportunities Do Not Mature While You Wait

*Speed to insight. Speed to test. Speed to decision.*

FOUNDER VOICE | KRUGER PHIRI | MARKETS & GROWTH | 22 AUGUST 2026  

## 1 \| The market does not pay interest on hesitation

Founders often discuss opportunities as if markets will preserve them politely until management feels emotionally ready. The opportunity is filed, circulated, discussed and scheduled for another assessment. Meanwhile customers change, competitors learn and technology becomes cheaper. By the time the board agrees to "explore the potential", the potential has already opened an account with someone else.

Do not misunderstand the argument. Moving quickly is not the same as taking reckless risk. Speed is useful when it shortens the time between a question, an evidence-gathering experiment and an informed decision. It is dangerous when it simply accelerates spending. The disciplined company does not worship speed. It refuses unnecessary waiting.

The World Bank's Global Findex 2025 report is based on surveys of approximately 148,000 adults across 141 economies, conducted in 2024. It documents rapid changes in financial access and digital connectivity and reports that 79% of adults worldwide had a financial account. \[1\] That statistic is global, not an estimate of every African market. Its relevance here is not a slogan about universal fintech opportunity. It shows that the infrastructure of customer behaviour can change substantially and that founders must work with evidence that has dates and locations attached.

A market map produced two years ago may be a useful starting point, but it is not a current customer interview. A competitor analysis without testing current prices and product experiences can mislead. A partner's enthusiasm is not proof of purchasing intent. Opportunity needs active verification. It does not mature in a folder labelled "strategic prospects".

## 2 \| Why founders postpone useful decisions

Sometimes the company lacks money or skills. Sometimes regulation genuinely requires patience. But delay also comes from fear of rejection, internal politics, excessive approval layers and the preference for grand launches over small tests. Teams would rather debate a national rollout than ask twenty prospective customers whether they will pay for a basic version. A national rollout sounds visionary. Twenty honest conversations might hurt someone's feelings.

## 3 \| Build a faster evidence cycle

Start with a narrow claim: a defined customer in a defined location will pay a defined price for a defined outcome. List what would falsify that claim. Conduct interviews that explore the existing problem, alternatives and purchasing authority. Show a basic prototype or service description. Request a concrete next step, such as a paid pilot or conditional procurement process. Record refusals and objections. A polite compliment is not traction.

Put an explicit time limit on exploration, suitable for the stakes. A two-week diagnostic may be enough to identify whether a simple professional service solves an urgent problem. A regulated financial product will require a much longer compliance and testing path. The point is not a universal deadline. It is to prevent the organisation from postponing the decision indefinitely because no one defined what evidence would be sufficient.

## 4 \| Test cheaply before betting heavily

Small reversible experiments protect capital. Before leasing a distribution warehouse, validate order frequency, route density and payment terms with a limited area. Before constructing a facility, test demand, feedstock security and permitted site options. Before building a platform with every conceivable feature, determine which function customers will actually use and pay for. The experiment should produce a decision, not merely an interesting presentation.

The World Bank's May 2025 Nigeria Development Update discusses economic reforms and the need to translate macroeconomic progress into inclusive growth. It is a national analytical report, not a recommendation to enter any specific Nigerian sector. \[2\] Businesses evaluating a market should combine such macro analysis with sector licensing, currency exposure, customer economics and local counterparties. Large population numbers are not substitutes for distribution capability or reliable collections.

Rapid discovery also requires governance. Define a maximum amount at risk, what customer information may be processed, the minimum legal approvals and the stop conditions. Innovation does not exempt a company from privacy, labour or consumer rules. A pilot that creates uncontrolled liabilities is not lean. It is simply under-governed.

## 5 \| The decision you avoid is also a decision

A deferred decision has a cost. Calculate it where possible: lost contribution from delayed launch, duplicated staff effort, price changes, contract expiry or missed procurement windows. Also calculate the cost of acting too soon. Good judgement compares both, instead of assuming delay is free and acceleration is brave. Sometimes waiting for a permit is responsible. Sometimes waiting for another committee meeting is avoidance disguised as prudence.

Make a decision log with four possible outcomes: proceed, test, defer pending named evidence or stop. Every deferral needs an owner, a specific evidence gap and a revisit date. "Revisit in due course" is not a decision. It is the corporate equivalent of leaving a message unread and hoping the sender disappears.

## 6 \| Market timing without the mythology

Founders love stories about being first. First is only useful if the business can deliver a product customers want at economics it can sustain. Being early and unready can destroy capital; being later with better distribution can still create value. The relevant question is whether your timing, capability and financing match the market's current problem. Evaluate switching costs, incumbent responses, regulatory pace and customer trust, not just market growth headlines.

Suppose digital payments are spreading in a market. A merchant-services company should not assume that increased account ownership guarantees margin. It must examine active usage, transaction fees, agent economics, fraud loss, settlement reliability and dispute resolution. The global Findex figure is a context measure, not a projected income statement. Treating it as one would be an expensive act of enthusiasm.

Develop a twenty-question opportunity assessment specific to your sector. Five questions each on the customer, route to market, economics and execution constraints. Keep the evidence concise and visible. Rank the uncertainties by their capacity to invalidate the venture. Investigate the largest one first, even if it is less enjoyable than creating a logo.

## 7 \| The next thirty days

In week one, identify three opportunities already stalled in the organisation. Name the decision required and the assumption blocking it. In week two, speak with actual buyers, regulators or commercial counterparties as appropriate. In week three, run the lowest-cost lawful test that could change the decision. In week four, decide whether to proceed, redesign, defer for specific evidence or stop. Report results without recasting weak demand as "strong market interest".

Track time to first customer evidence, time from evidence to decision, cost per validated learning and the proportion of experiments that lead to a clear choice. An abandoned weak idea can be a successful experiment if it prevents a large poor investment. A successful pilot that is never converted into an operating plan can still be wasted effort.

## 8 \| Final provocation

Opportunity rarely arrives with a certificate declaring its expiry date. Sometimes it disappears quietly as customers adopt a competing solution. Sometimes it becomes unprofitable as costs change. Sometimes regulations make the original model impossible. The founder's duty is to notice changes, test them intelligently and decide before information becomes obsolete.

Stop holding opportunities hostage to ceremonial planning. Put evidence into the room earlier. Give decisions owners and deadlines. Accept that a clear "no" is sometimes more valuable than months of motivational uncertainty. The market does not reward how long you admired an opportunity. It rewards what you built, what customers chose and what you managed to sustain.
