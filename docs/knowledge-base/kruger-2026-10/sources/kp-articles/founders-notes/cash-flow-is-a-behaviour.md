# Cash Flow Is a Behaviour, Not a Spreadsheet

*Cash flow pressure is rarely a math problem. It is a discipline problem.*

FOUNDER VOICE | KRUGER PHIRI | CAPITAL & FUNDING | 14 SEPTEMBER 2026  

## 1 \| The bank account is not impressed by profit

There is a special kind of business confidence that appears in a boardroom after someone announces that revenue is growing by double digits. Everyone smiles. The founder requests a bigger office. The finance manager quietly wonders whether payroll will clear on Friday. This is why I resist the idea that cash flow is merely a spreadsheet problem. A spreadsheet can report your habits; it cannot repair them while you continue spending tomorrow's receipts today.

Profit and cash answer different questions. Profit concerns performance measured under applicable accounting policies. Cash concerns money actually available to meet obligations when they fall due. A credit sale can produce recorded revenue before the customer pays. Stock consumes cash before it is sold. A capital purchase may require a large immediate payment while its expense is recognised over time. A tax obligation does not disappear because the chief executive describes the business as high growth.

IAS 7 distinguishes cash flows from operating, investing and financing activities. That discipline matters because a company can seem liquid only because it has borrowed, sold assets or delayed suppliers. Those sources may be legitimate, but they should not be confused with repeatable operating cash generation. \[1\] It is not a moral failure to finance working capital. It is a management failure to discover the need three days before salaries are due.

A founder who checks the bank balance twice a day but never reviews receivables ageing is watching the dashboard while ignoring the road. A founder who signs every customer contract without understanding payment terms is selling goods and financing customers at the same time. Those are two businesses. They have different risks. Charge for both, or stop pretending the second one is free.

## 2 \| Revenue without collection is expensive theatre

Consider an illustrative order worth R1 million with a 25% gross margin. Suppose the business must pay suppliers R750,000 before the customer settles an invoice 90 days later. The sale might be profitable on paper, yet the company must find R750,000 in cash during the collection gap. If three such orders overlap, the temporary supplier funding requirement could reach R2.25 million before operating overheads. This is a worked example, not a real company result. It demonstrates how successful selling can create a cash crisis.

## 3 \| The behaviour behind the numbers

Cash management begins before the invoice is issued. It begins when sales promises credit terms without consulting finance; when procurement buys too much because the discount feels generous; when the founder treats VAT or payroll deductions as free short-term funding; and when managers refuse to call overdue customers because collections might make the relationship uncomfortable. The arithmetic is not mysterious. The avoidance behaviour is expensive.

Set a minimum cash discipline. Prepare a 13-week cash forecast every week. Distinguish contracted, probable and speculative receipts. Reconcile opening cash to bank statements. Assign a named owner to every overdue balance. Compare purchase commitments with approved budgets and the cash calendar. Review what changed from last week's forecast and why. A forecast that is always wrong for the same reason is not uncertainty. It is a management signal being ignored.

Define three classes of expenditure: essential to continue operating safely and legally, necessary for contracted delivery, and discretionary. Fund them in that order during pressure periods. This is not a universal legal priority rule, and statutory or contractual obligations require qualified advice. It is a management lens for asking whether the business is protecting its ability to trade. The executive team must never use "cash discipline" as a euphemism for withholding money legally due to others.

## 4 \| The customer you are afraid to challenge

A slow-paying customer can be more dangerous than a difficult one. The difficult customer announces the problem. The slow payer often arrives with compliments, a future pipeline and a request for just one more extension. The founder responds: "But they are strategic." Strategic to whom? Their treasury department seems to be enjoying an interest-free facility financed by your anxiety.

Price terms properly. Verify creditworthiness before large exposures. Link milestone billing to accepted deliverables. Offer early-payment incentives only where the economics work. Require deposits for custom production when commercial norms permit. Use written escalation rules before a debt becomes stale. If customers resist, quantify the financing cost rather than accusing them of bad faith. Protect genuine relationships through clarity, not silence.

IFC's 2024 partnership with C2FO was designed to expand supply-chain financing for African smaller enterprises by helping convert accepted invoices into earlier cash. That is a documented example of a financing mechanism aimed at a working-capital problem, not proof that every invoice should be discounted. \[2\] Financing can bridge a timing gap; it does not cure weak margins or persistent non-payment.

## 5 \| Working capital should have an owner

Every expanding business should understand three basic periods: how long inventory sits before sale, how long customers take to pay and how long suppliers allow before payment. Together they shape the cash conversion cycle. The formula is simple: inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days. The interpretation requires care. Longer supplier terms can improve near-term cash, but aggressive delays can damage supply continuity and reputation. The purpose is not to squeeze the smallest participant in the chain. It is to design a viable commercial system.

Create one weekly working-capital meeting that lasts no more than 30 minutes. Sales explains major invoicing and collection exceptions. Operations explains stock and project work in progress. Procurement explains committed payments. Finance reconciles the forecast and identifies the two or three decisions needed. The meeting ends with names and dates. If the outcome is simply "we must improve cash flow", schedule another meeting to discuss why the previous meeting was useless.

## 6 \| The uncomfortable shareholder question

Owners are entitled to seek returns. They are not entitled to confuse company money with personal money. Establish documented rules for dividends, director loans, expenses and approvals. Separate discretionary distributions from the liquidity required to operate safely. Review whether bonuses reward billed revenue rather than collected contribution. Examine commissions where a salesperson closes a technically loss-making contract and still celebrates a win.

An illustrative commercial test is useful. Suppose a service line invoices R200,000, pays R120,000 in direct costs, incurs R40,000 in attributable overhead and waits four months to collect. Its operating contribution before financing cost is R40,000, not R80,000. If the customer has a material risk of non-payment, the apparent gain is even less secure. Do not invent a precise cost of capital; apply the business's actual borrowing rate and agreed payment terms.

A finance function cannot rescue a culture that rewards fantasy. Leaders must make cash visibility safe, even when the message is unpleasant. The person reporting a shortfall early is not the villain. The leader who responds by asking for a nicer chart might be.

## 7 \| Seven habits for the next ninety days

First, invoice immediately on completion of contractual milestones. Second, resolve disputes while facts and relationships are fresh. Third, maintain an accurate aged-debtors report. Fourth, compare actual collections with the prior week's commitments. Fifth, review all recurring costs quarterly, including the beloved subscriptions nobody remembers approving. Sixth, maintain a minimum liquidity policy appropriate to the enterprise's risks and volatility. Seventh, require commercial decisions above a material threshold to state the expected cash timing alongside the accounting margin.

Measure behaviour, not simply a month-end balance. Track days sales outstanding, overdue receivables by age, cash forecast accuracy, inventory held without demand, gross margin by customer and the cash required to complete confirmed orders. No single metric is a magic answer. Their power comes from explaining why cash moved and whether management can act before the next shortage.

The US Small Business Administration's April 2025 guidance highlights budgeting, bookkeeping, borrowing and financial planning as practical capabilities for small businesses. That is sensible advice, though it is written for a US context and not a substitute for South African accounting or tax advice. \[3\] The universal lesson is operational: numbers become useful when someone takes a decision because of them.

## 8 \| Final provocation

The spreadsheet does not choose your customers, approve your supplier payments or withdraw money from the account. People do. Stop presenting cash flow as a mysterious weather event. It is shaped by commercial terms, process discipline, governance and sometimes genuine external shocks. Distinguish the shock from the self-inflicted wound.

A profitable business can fail because it runs out of cash. A cash-rich business can destroy value through bad investment. Both deserve competent management. The founder's job is not to perform surprise at the bank balance. It is to create habits that make bad surprises less frequent, more visible and more manageable. Cash flow is a behaviour. The spreadsheet is merely the witness.
