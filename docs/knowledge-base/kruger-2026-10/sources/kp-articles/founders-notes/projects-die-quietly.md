# Projects Die Quietly, Before They Collapse

*Most project failures start in preparation, not construction.*

FOUNDER VOICE | KRUGER PHIRI | PROJECT DEVELOPMENT | 07 JULY 2026  

## 1 \| Failure begins while everyone is still smiling

Public conversations about failed infrastructure projects usually begin when a contractor abandons a site, a completion date slips dramatically or a newspaper photographs a half-built structure. By that stage, the decisive mistakes may be years old. The wrong site was selected. Demand was exaggerated. Authority to acquire land was assumed. The design was insufficiently developed. Political urgency was mistaken for commercial readiness. Construction is when the evidence becomes visible; it is not always when the damage begins.

The quiet death of a project sounds professional. "Land arrangements are being finalised." "Off-take discussions are advanced." "The environmental process is underway." Each statement may be true, but none tells an investment committee whether a critical condition has been met. The grammar is reassuring because the risk has not been assigned to anyone. This is how delay becomes a permanent organisational hobby.

A project is not a document, a model or a hopeful announcement. It is a chain of dependent rights, engineering choices, financial commitments, contracts, permits and operating capabilities. If one indispensable link is missing, the rest of the plan may be incapable of functioning. Leaders must stop confusing the existence of an attractive project concept with authority to proceed.

The World Bank's PPP Reference Guide describes a project cycle that moves from identification through appraisal, structuring, procurement and contract management. The guide is oriented to public-private partnerships, and its legal details vary by country and project. Its central discipline remains useful: development and implementation are distinct stages with different questions and evidence. \[1\]

## 2 \| Four quiet warning signals

Watch for repeated postponement of one prerequisite, an unexplained movement in capital costs, a revenue forecast unsupported by commercial evidence, and a delivery timetable that assumes every approval will be obtained on the first attempt. Any one signal can be manageable. Together they suggest that the project is advancing administratively while standing still substantively.

## 3 \| The land and permission trap

For a tower, factory or logistics park, site control is not a footnote. A map coordinate is not a lease. A conversation with a landowner is not authority to build. A preliminary planning view is not a permit. Before significant commitment, determine the legal status of the land, applicable restrictions, environmental or planning requirements, access rights, utility availability and construction constraints. Use qualified local advisers where rights are complex.

Consider an illustrative telecommunications site intended for three tenants. The model projects rent from multiple occupiers, but only one operator has expressed non-binding interest. The design is technically plausible, yet land access has not been secured. A sponsor may proudly present 25 years of forecast revenue. The real commercial questions begin much earlier: which party will sign, what triggers rent, who finances utilities, and what happens if permits are delayed? The numerical horizon does not cure the missing contract.

## 4 \| Feasibility is a decision process, not a paid ornament

A useful feasibility study challenges whether the project should proceed and under what conditions. It assesses demand, technical options, costs, execution capacity, environmental and social issues, procurement, operating economics and downside scenarios. Commissioning a study solely to confirm a preselected outcome defeats its purpose. If the brief effectively says "prove the chairman is correct", a firm may produce a beautiful report and a terrible investment.

Every critical assumption needs a source, owner, date and test. Market demand requires buyer evidence or a defensible independent assessment. Costs require appropriate estimates and contingencies. Schedule requires realistic permissions and supply-chain lead times. Financing requires credible sources of capital and terms. The Project Management Institute's 2025 work stresses business outcomes beyond the traditional project measures of budget, scope and schedule. It is a reminder that an asset delivered on time but unable to generate its intended value is still a strategic disappointment. \[2\]

The inconvenient conclusion is that some projects should be stopped before they become construction projects. Early cancellation can be responsible stewardship of capital. A feasibility assessment that recommends "do not proceed" may be the most valuable study in the portfolio.

## 5 \| The sponsor must stop outsourcing reality

Consultants are useful. Engineers, lawyers, environmental specialists, financiers and project managers add essential expertise. But the sponsor remains accountable for integrating their findings and making an informed decision. One consultant cannot assume that the next consultant has resolved the interface. When studies disagree, document the conflict and obtain a decision. When permits remain uncertain, identify how the schedule and economics change. When a stakeholder objects, treat the issue as a risk to understand, not as an inconvenience to dismiss.

Establish an evidence register divided into five conditions: commercial demand; site and legal rights; technical and environmental readiness; procurement and delivery capacity; and financing and governance. Grade each item green only when the document exists and its validity is checked. Amber means a named gap with an agreed mitigation. Red means a prerequisite is absent or unacceptable. Do not change the colour because the launch event has been booked.

## 6 \| The danger of heroic schedules

A schedule with no contingency is not automatically ambitious. It may simply be unserious. Identify the critical path and dependencies that cannot run concurrently. Test the effects of site acquisition, imported equipment, utility connection, public approvals and commissioning. Note which work can be advanced lawfully and commercially before full financing and which cannot. The fastest route is often to resolve the longest-lead uncertainty first, not to demand that contractors "work harder" after mobilisation.

Use milestone gates with explicit authority. Concept gate: is the problem real and the proposed solution plausible? Feasibility gate: do evidence and alternatives support investment? Readiness gate: are site, permits, commercial terms and delivery plans sufficient? Financial-close gate: are funds, conditions and covenants aligned? Construction gate: is the site and scope buildable? Commissioning gate: is the asset safe, accepted and capable of operating? Gates are not bureaucracy when they prevent paying for work that cannot legally or practically continue.

Stakeholders also matter. A project may be financially attractive but socially untenable. Community participation, legitimate compensation, safety and environmental compliance belong in preparation, not in the crisis-response budget. Treating affected people as an obstacle is both ethically poor and commercially shortsighted.

## 7 \| A one-page project mortality test

Before the next investment committee, ask: what single missing item could stop the project? Who owns it? What evidence shows completion? What does failure cost? Which party can change the outcome? How long can we wait before the economics become unacceptable? Repeat the test for demand, land, permits, engineering, financing and operations. Then ask an outsider to find the assumptions that everyone has stopped questioning.

Prepare three cost views: preliminary estimate, risk-adjusted control budget and funding requirement including realistic timing. Do not present historical sponsor expenditure as liquid funding available to close. Separate contract prices from unpriced assumptions. Include operational readiness, not merely construction completion. A plant without staff, feedstock or a buyer is an expensive building. A tower without lease activation may be an expensive mast.

## 8 \| Final provocation

Projects die quietly because organisations reward visible momentum over resolved risk. A ribbon-cutting photo is not evidence of bankability. A signed memorandum is not always an off-take agreement. A twenty-five-year model is not twenty-five years of income. The project survives when the sponsor forces each assumption to face evidence and accepts the possibility that the right decision is to stop.

Do the difficult work while it is still relatively cheap. Confront the missing land right, weak demand estimate, implausible timetable and incomplete funding plan before excavators arrive. If you cannot explain why a project is ready, please do not announce that it is shovel-ready. The shovel is innocent. The governance is not.
