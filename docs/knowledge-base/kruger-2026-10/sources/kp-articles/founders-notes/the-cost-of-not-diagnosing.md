# The Cost of Not Diagnosing the Real Problem

*Treating symptoms keeps the business sick and expensive.*

FOUNDER VOICE | KRUGER PHIRI | STRATEGY & EXECUTION | 05 OCTOBER 2026  

## 1 \| The expensive comfort of treating symptoms

A founder sees falling sales and demands more advertising. Leads rise, conversion remains weak, cash deteriorates and the response is another campaign. A manufacturer sees late orders and adds overtime. Overtime costs rise, defects increase and the bottleneck stays exactly where it was. A service company loses customers and orders the team to be "more customer centric". Everyone attends a workshop. The customers continue leaving. These are not unusual mysteries. They are examples of action without diagnosis.

The problem with visible symptoms is that they compete loudly for attention. The underlying cause is often less theatrical: confused pricing, poorly defined customer selection, a defective process handoff, unsustainable payment terms, weak management information or a reward system that encourages the wrong behaviour. Executives like solutions they can announce immediately. Root causes are less cooperative. They ask the executive to admit that the organisation has misunderstood itself.

Diagnosis means establishing what is happening, where, to whom, since when and through which mechanism. It is not an elaborate excuse to delay necessary action. Some emergencies require immediate containment while investigation continues. A leaking machine should be made safe before a committee debates its root cause. But containment is not cure. The organisation must know which is which.

The OECD's 2023 governance principles connect strategy, monitoring, risk management and internal control with leadership responsibilities. They do not mandate a particular diagnostic tool for every private business. They do support the basic proposition that decision-makers need reliable information and mechanisms for correction. \[1\] Leadership without diagnosis is merely confidence unsupported by inspection.

## 2 \| The wrong problem gets the biggest budget

Suppose a business reports lower profit and assumes labour cost is too high. It reduces headcount. Delivery slows, repeat work increases and customers leave. Later analysis reveals that a small group of contracts had been consistently underpriced. The salary reduction treated the accounting symptom while worsening the commercial cause. This is a hypothetical case, but the causal structure is common enough to deserve deliberate testing before irreversible decisions.

## 3 \| Begin with a question that can be falsified

Instead of "Why is our team underperforming?", ask "Which stage in the order-to-cash process produced the greatest deterioration in the last three months?" Instead of "Do we need a new sales director?", ask "Did lead quality, conversion, average order value, contribution margin or collection speed change?" Specific questions create evidence. Broad accusations create defensive meetings.

Map the process from customer enquiry to cash received. Identify the owners, handoffs, data and control points. Compare high-performing cases with weak-performing cases. Interview staff who handle exceptions, not only executives who receive summaries. Review a sample of transactions and test the numbers against the source systems. Averages can hide concentration. A growing customer count may conceal falling contribution per customer. A positive monthly sales trend may conceal an expanding population of unpaid invoices.

## 4 \| Five whys, without the theatre

The "five whys" technique is a prompt to investigate successive causes, not a magical requirement to ask the question exactly five times. A late shipment occurred because production finished late. Production finished late because material was missing. Material was missing because purchasing placed an order late. Purchasing acted late because the demand signal was inaccurate. The forecast was inaccurate because sales and production used different definitions of confirmed demand. The useful fix is then a common forecast and order-control process, not another warning letter to the forklift driver.

Do not stop at the first explanation that flatters management. If the answer is "staff need training", ask whether instructions, incentives, workload, technology or supervisors make correct performance possible. If the answer is "customers are difficult", ask which contract terms and service promises create predictable conflict. A diagnosis should change what the organisation does. It should not merely produce a more sophisticated way to blame people.

The US Small Business Administration's guidance on business finance highlights balance-sheet discipline and cost-benefit analysis. Those are not substitutes for diagnosis, and the guidance is tailored to a US context. They are practical reminders that decisions about spending and financing should be grounded in identifiable costs, liabilities and expected benefits. \[2\]

## 5 \| The diagnosis-to-decision chain

Require each proposed intervention to answer four questions. What is the observed problem? What evidence identifies the most probable mechanism? What action targets that mechanism? Which metric will tell us whether it worked? Place the intervention under a trial period where possible. A digital system is not justified because competitors use one. It is justified when a specific process defect is established and the expected benefit exceeds the full cost and risk of adoption.

Suppose customer complaints cluster around incorrect invoices. A new customer-relationship system might not help if sales promises are entered correctly but the billing team uses outdated price schedules. The narrower fix may be authoritative pricing records, controlled changes and invoice validation. Before purchasing software, map one hundred recent failures and classify the origin of each. The number one hundred is an illustrative sampling design, not a scientifically universal minimum. Choose a suitable sample with qualified assistance where the stakes warrant it.

## 6 \| Measure the cost of indecision

Diagnosis is not free. Neither is acting blindly. Develop a decision-value estimate by comparing the cost of a limited investigation with the potential loss avoided and the decision it enables. For example, suppose a company is considering R2 million in additional marketing. A diagnostic costing R100,000 identifies that its sales follow-up system fails to contact qualified prospects promptly. If the business can validate that a process fix has a higher expected return, the diagnostic may save substantial misallocated spend. The figures are illustrative and should never be presented as an achieved result.

Do not overdiagnose every trivial choice. Match investigation effort to reversibility, consequence and uncertainty. A minor website headline can be tested rapidly. A long-term infrastructure contract, major acquisition or organisational restructuring requires deeper diligence. Good leaders know when to move quickly and when speed without understanding is simply expensive impatience.

Write down competing hypotheses. Assign each the evidence that would support or contradict it. Review inconvenient observations. Distinguish causal proof from correlation. If sales fell after a new competitor entered, the competitor may matter. Pricing, reliability or customer service may also have changed. A single chart rarely identifies a cause by itself.

## 7 \| A founder's diagnostic dashboard

Track six categories together: demand quality, unit economics, operational flow, cash conversion, people capability and control failures. For each, choose a few measures tied to a real decision. Revenue by itself does not diagnose demand quality. Margin by itself does not show working-capital stress. Employee headcount does not demonstrate capability. Compliance certificates do not prove controls operate. The point is not to drown management in metrics. It is to connect apparently separate symptoms.

Then choose one costly recurring problem and run a two-week investigation. Freeze speculative solutions. Collect a baseline. Trace the process and talk to people who do the work. Test competing explanations. Recommend one bounded intervention, name its owner and set the review date. If the intervention fails, do not defend it because senior management sponsored it. Learn and revise.

## 8 \| Final provocation

The most expensive consultancy report is not always the one with the highest fee. Sometimes it is the report management never commissioned, because the board was certain it already understood the problem. A founder who prefers reassuring explanations to inconvenient evidence is volunteering to pay tuition to reality, repeatedly.

Stop confusing immediate action with competent action. When the business is bleeding cash, losing customers or missing commitments, investigate the mechanism while containing the harm. Treat the disease, not the headline. If the same expensive problem keeps returning under a different project name, the organisation may not have a problem with execution at all. It may have a problem with refusing to diagnose itself.
