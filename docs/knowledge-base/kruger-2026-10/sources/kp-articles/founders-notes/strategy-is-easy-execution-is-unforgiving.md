# Strategy Is Easy. Execution Is Unforgiving.

*Plans do not fail on paper. They fail in people, process and leadership.*

FOUNDER VOICE | KRUGER PHIRI | STRATEGY & EXECUTION | 17 AUGUST 2026  

## 1 \| The strategy document cannot execute itself

There are organisations with strategic plans so elegantly designed that they deserve museum lighting. The pages announce transformation, agility, excellence and stakeholder value. Then Monday arrives. Nobody owns the first decision. Procurement is still waiting. Operations has not seen the plan. Finance says the assumptions were never approved. By Friday, another steering committee has been scheduled. At least the calendar is performing.

Strategy is a choice about where to compete, whom to serve, how value is created and what the organisation will refuse to pursue. Execution is the uncomfortable system of turning those choices into funded work, authority, measurable results and corrective decisions. Confusing one with the other is expensive. A strategic ambition without operating consequences is not yet a strategy in action. It is a sentence with good posture.

The Project Management Institute's 2025 Pulse of the Profession focuses on business acumen as a differentiator for project professionals. It reports 66% of surveyed professionals had moderate proficiency, 18% high and 16% low. Those categories concern the survey's proficiency assessment; they do not prove that 82% of all projects are failing. \[1\] The practical point is sound: delivery professionals must understand commercial outcomes, not merely complete activities according to the schedule.

A founder should be able to describe the strategy in a few sentences and then point to the operating decisions it has changed. Which customer segments will receive additional effort? Which product will be stopped? Which projects will have capital withheld until prerequisites are met? Which management incentives will change? If nothing changes, the strategy exists mainly to decorate an offsite retreat.

## 2 \| Ambiguity is a management choice

Consider a company promising to double revenue by entering two new markets. The chief executive assumes sales will lead. Sales assumes operations will create local capacity. Operations assumes legal will resolve licensing. Legal assumes the decision has not been approved. Each manager is busy. No one is accountable for market entry. The company has not encountered an unforeseen obstacle; it has purchased ambiguity with its own budget.

## 3 \| Give every ambition a decision owner

Translate every strategic priority into one clear outcome, a deadline, an accountable person, allocated resources, a few leading indicators and a stop rule. The accountable person must have enough authority to make the work happen. Responsibility without authority is a management prank. Authority without accountability is a governance problem. Pair them deliberately.

If the priority is expanding a professional-service offering, the outcome is not "increase market visibility". It might be a defined number of paid discovery engagements converted into signed, profitable delivery agreements within an agreed period. Leading indicators may include qualified leads, proposal acceptance, delivery capacity and payment terms. The stop rule might trigger when acquisition cost exceeds an approved ceiling for several reporting periods. Adjust thresholds to the business's economics. Never copy invented benchmarks into a real budget.

The OECD's 2023 corporate-governance principles identify strategy, budgets, performance objectives, monitoring and oversight of major investments as key board responsibilities. Public-company rules do not automatically govern every private founder-led enterprise, but the allocation of duties is instructive. The board should challenge whether management's delivery system supports the approved direction. \[2\]

## 4 \| Kill the meeting that produces no decision

A good operating cadence creates time for decisions, not theatrical status recitals. At the weekly level, examine the handful of risks and results that can still be changed. Monthly, reconsider resources and assumptions. Quarterly, review whether the strategic choices remain sound. Record decisions, owners and dates in one place. If the same problem appears three times without action, the problem is no longer information. It is leadership.

Do not confuse a red-amber-green dashboard with control. A project can remain green because nobody wants the embarrassment of amber. Ask what evidence supports the colour, what would falsify it and who has authority to stop spending. Encourage managers to report a failing assumption early. Punishing bad news does not improve results. It improves the skill of concealing bad news.

The strongest execution teams distinguish commitments from hopes. They report contractual milestones, customer acceptance and cash consequences. When a supplier misses a delivery, they assess the critical path and recovery options rather than simply writing "supplier engaged". Engagement is not a milestone. Neither is positive energy.

## 5 \| Trade-offs expose whether you mean it

Most strategy failures begin with leaders refusing to choose. The company will pursue every sector, satisfy every customer, innovate continuously, reduce cost immediately and increase quality without additional resources. It also wants everyone to be happy. This is not a strategy. It is a collection of incompatible wishes with a PowerPoint licence.

Make trade-offs visible. Identify the work to stop to fund the work that matters. Reassign staff rather than merely adding responsibilities to already overloaded teams. When an opportunity appears, demand a comparison with the current portfolio. Which approved commitment will be delayed if this new initiative proceeds? Who accepts that consequence? What evidence justifies the switch? The discipline may appear restrictive. It is what makes priorities credible.

A practical instrument is the decision register. Each important decision states the question, options, evidence, chosen course, owner, deadline and review trigger. This is particularly useful for cross-functional work where memory becomes political. A decision written down reduces the temptation to rewrite history when results disappoint.

## 6 \| Results matter more than activity

Suppose a project team completes 95% of planned tasks but the product cannot be used because a critical approval is missing. The task-completion percentage may be arithmetically accurate and strategically useless. The true outcome is delayed availability. Build measures around what customers and funders receive: commissioned assets, service availability, collected cash, validated quality and reduced risk. Track activity as a means to those outcomes, not as a substitute.

Leaders should ask four questions each month. Which outcome moved? Which assumption changed? Which constraint is now binding? Which decision cannot wait? This is a smaller and more demanding agenda than a hundred-slide update. It leaves fewer places for ambiguity to hide.

Execution also requires institutional memory. Capture lessons from missed deadlines and budget variations while the facts are still available. Change standards, templates, procurement conditions or skills accordingly. A retrospective that ends with "communicate better next time" has taught the organisation very little.

## 7 \| A thirty-day execution audit

Week one: choose the three highest-value strategic commitments and name the person answerable for each. Week two: trace the cash, decisions, suppliers and approvals on the actual delivery path. Week three: inspect every dependency that can stop the critical outcome. Week four: run a live review in which the team must approve, pause, accelerate or cancel specific actions. Record the cost of each choice and the next proof point.

Score your readiness on five elements: strategic focus, decision rights, funded capacity, reliable evidence and correction speed. An organisation with excellent focus but no authorised spending plan is not halfway to success. It may be halfway to another delay. Review the weakest dependency first.

Do not demand certainty where uncertainty is genuine. Use staged commitments. Release resources when prerequisites are met. Conduct low-cost tests to retire key unknowns. Escalate legal, environmental, safety and procurement obligations to qualified people rather than pretending speed excuses compliance. Good execution is not reckless acceleration. It is disciplined progress through visible constraints.

## 8 \| Final provocation

Strategy is easy only relative to what follows. The hard part is explaining what will stop, who will be accountable, how the work will be funded and which disappointing facts will change the plan. Execution punishes ambiguity without asking whether your slide design was premium.

The organisation that wins is not necessarily the one with the loudest ambition. It is the one that makes more competent decisions, resolves constraints earlier and learns before the cost of learning becomes catastrophic. Stop asking whether staff have read the strategy. Ask whether the strategy has changed their decisions. If it has not, print fewer copies and do the actual work.
