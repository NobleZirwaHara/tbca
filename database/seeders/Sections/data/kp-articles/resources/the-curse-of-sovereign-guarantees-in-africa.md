Africa is routinely told that its development ambitions exceed its fiscal capacity. Governments want to build railways, power plants, industrial parks, refineries and export infrastructure. Lenders ask for bankable revenue, adequate security and, quite often, a sovereign guarantee. Treasury officials warn about debt ceilings. International lenders warn about fiscal risks. Projects are postponed. Citizens inherit the familiar result: expensive imports, unreliable infrastructure and productive assets that remain drawings in feasibility studies.

But the argument is too important for slogans. A sovereign guarantee is neither free money nor automatically a fully disbursed sovereign loan. It is a legally defined promise that can move credit risk from a project to the state. Whether that promise becomes an immediate accounting liability, a statistical contingent liability or an item restricted by a financing programme depends on three different rulebooks. Confusing those rulebooks makes for poor decisions. \[1\]\[2\]\[3\]

The question for African presidents is therefore not whether to issue guarantees indiscriminately. It is whether they understand them well enough to use them selectively, transparently and competitively for projects that expand national income. A continent that cannot distinguish a risk instrument from a cash borrowing may reject valuable investment. A continent that ignores the risks may repeat some of its costliest financing disasters.

## 1. A sovereign guarantee is a promise, not a payment

A sovereign guarantee is an undertaking, ordinarily issued by a state through an authorised ministry or treasury, to meet a specified obligation if a named borrower fails to do so or if another agreed trigger occurs. In a basic loan guarantee, three parties matter: the borrower, the lender and the government guarantor. The government is not necessarily receiving the loan proceeds. It is accepting a conditional exposure so the lender will finance the project at all, advance more, extend the maturity or reduce the price. \[5\]\[9\]

Consider an electricity transmission company seeking US\$100 million for an export-linked line. The company may own the asset, collect tariffs and owe the lender. A government might guarantee only the first US\$20 million of certain debt-service losses. That is a different legal and economic proposition from the government borrowing US\$100 million itself. It is also different from the government guaranteeing every obligation of the company without a cap.

There is no single standard sovereign guarantee. The instrument can cover principal only, scheduled interest, specified political risks, payment defaults by a public utility, termination amounts or other contractually defined exposures. The issuer's legal authority, appropriation rules, public-debt legislation, parliamentary oversight and contractual wording determine whether the promise is valid and enforceable. A lender may still reject an apparently generous guarantee if the state has a weak repayment record or the promise is legally defective. A minister's signature is not the same thing as bankable credit.

Guarantees can help citizens when they reduce financing costs and accelerate useful assets. They can hurt citizens when officials use them to conceal fiscal support, favour connected borrowers or rescue projects without credible cash flows. Their purpose is credit enhancement, not financial magic.

## 2. Who appointed the IMF as Africa's financial headmaster?

The anger behind this question is understandable. When a country depends on an IMF-supported programme, agreed borrowing ceilings can materially constrain what its government may guarantee. The Fund's Debt Limits Policy allows quantitative conditions on public and publicly guaranteed borrowing in response to debt vulnerabilities. For some countries facing high debt-distress risk, programme design may establish a zero ceiling on new non-concessional external borrowing, subject to specified exceptions or adjustments. Other countries face different or no debt ceilings. \[3\]\[4\]

That is substantial influence. It is not evidence of a universal legal prohibition on African sovereign guarantees. The IMF does not acquire constitutional authority over every African treasury by virtue of being the IMF. Constraints arise through a member country's financing programme and the conditions agreed under it. Countries that do not have such a programme are not automatically subject to the same ceilings. Domestic constitutions and public-finance laws can impose separate restrictions.

The difficult political question remains: can a financing framework designed to reduce debt distress also prevent a well-structured, export-earning asset from reaching financial close? Yes, that tension is possible. A guarantee may mobilise private capital today while leaving the state with an uncertain repayment obligation tomorrow. A debt limit cannot assess every project solely by whether it comes with a guarantee. It must also consider risk allocation, future foreign-exchange earnings, tariff affordability, project execution and the country's capacity to absorb failure.

That is precisely where African governments should negotiate with evidence rather than indignation. Bring independently tested feasibility studies, project-specific stress tests, capped exposures, transparent contracts, reserve accounts and financing alternatives to programme discussions. Ask for a justified exception where permitted, a better-calibrated ceiling, a concessional guarantee facility or a structure that transfers less risk to the sovereign. The Fund's own guidance contemplates country-specific debt conditionality, not a single rule mechanically imposed everywhere. \[3\]\[4\]

## 3. The conflict-of-interest allegation deserves evidence, not theatre

Is the IMF competing with private lenders? Its role in lending to countries and monitoring agreed programme commitments creates an obvious question about bargaining power and policy influence. The criticism that programme restrictions can favour caution over investment is legitimate and testable. Yet the available policy documents do not establish that the Fund earns a commercial benefit from stopping a specific African project or deliberately misclassifies guarantees to block rival capital. Such a charge would require transaction-level evidence, not inference from an unpopular restriction. \[3\]\[4\]

There are, however, real distributional consequences. An excessively cautious government can leave infrastructure users, exporters and potential employees paying the price of delay. An excessively permissive one can transfer commercial losses to taxpayers while lenders and project sponsors retain much of the upside. Both outcomes deserve scrutiny. Who benefits is a question to investigate deal by deal: examine advisory fees, financing margins, beneficial ownership, procurement decisions, insurance, guarantee charges and who carries first losses.

The appropriate demand is institutional symmetry: publish the methodology for risk assessment, publish the sovereign's maximum commitment, and show why one transaction passes while another fails. African finance ministers should be prepared to question assumptions. They must also be prepared to accept adverse findings when the project economics are weak.

## 4. The most important correction: there are three different ledgers

Much of the public debate begins with a category error. It treats the maximum face value of a guarantee, the liability recognised in accrual financial statements and the debt or contingent liability recorded in government statistics as if they were interchangeable. They are not.

First, the IMF's Government Finance Statistics Manual 2014 generally classifies a one-off guarantee as a contingent liability of the guarantor. The underlying debt remains attributed to the original debtor unless and until the guarantee is called. A distressed borrower's guarantee with a very high likelihood of being called can be treated differently. Standardised guarantee schemes, by contrast, generate provisions for expected calls in government statistics. The manual recommends showing the stock of publicly guaranteed debt as a memorandum item. \[1\]

Second, an accrual-reporting public-sector entity applying IPSAS 41 recognises qualifying financial guarantee contracts at inception. An exchange-transaction guarantee can initially be measured by the consideration received; a non-market or non-exchange guarantee normally uses fair value, with a loss-allowance approach where a reliable fair value cannot be established. Later measurement generally uses the higher of the required loss allowance and the remaining initial amount. The maximum contractual payout is disclosed as an exposure, not automatically booked in full as the initial liability. These points are explained in IPSASB staff's February 2026 publication, which explicitly notes that it is non-authoritative interpretive material. \[2\]

Third, IMF programme debt limits and debt-sustainability analyses may count or constrain newly contracted or publicly guaranteed obligations for surveillance and programme purposes, even when an accounting balance sheet recognises only an expected-loss liability. Those are prudential and policy measures, not proof that the Treasury received and spent the full guaranteed sum. \[3\]\[4\]

A fair debate must say which ledger it is criticising. If an official claims that every US\$100 million guarantee always means US\$100 million of cash debt is booked as a Treasury borrowing, request the governing standard and the exact journal entry. If another official insists that every guarantee must remain off balance sheet because no payment has occurred, that is equally unsafe under accrual accounting.

## 5. The house worth US\$100,000 and the loan worth US\$7,500

Let us follow a simple example. Mr X owns a house independently valued at US\$100,000. Bank Z considers a US\$10,000 request but approves only US\$7,500, secured by a mortgage over the house. Assume the advance is made in cash, ignore fees, interest accrual and opening balances, and assume Mr X retains ownership of the home.

Mr X records: debit cash US\$7,500; credit bank loan payable US\$7,500. Bank Z records: debit loan receivable US\$7,500; credit cash US\$7,500. The home remains Mr X's asset, subject to the security interest. Bank Z does not simply record the house as a US\$100,000 asset because it has a mortgage. The bank separately assesses expected credit losses on its loan under IFRS 9, including the effect of collateral and recoveries. \[13\]

### THE BANK EXAMPLE: SIMPLIFIED JOURNAL ENTRIES

| **Party**            | **Debit**                 | **Credit**             |
|----------------------|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Mr X                 | Cash US\$7,500            | Loan payable US\$7,500 |
| Bank Z               | Loan receivable US\$7,500 | Cash US\$7,500         |
| No disbursement case | No loan entry             | No loan entry          |

Now assume the bank rejects the application and advances US\$0. Without a separate enforceable loan or fee obligation, Mr X has no US\$7,500 loan payable, let alone a US\$100,000 debt. Delivering a title deed or valuation document for assessment does not by itself create loan proceeds. Any validly registered security instrument or separate contractual obligation must nevertheless be examined on its own terms.

This example proves one useful point: collateral value is not borrowed principal. It does not prove that a sovereign financial guarantee has no accounting value at issuance. A mortgage provided by the borrower to secure that borrower's own debt is fundamentally different from a government promising a third-party lender to cover another entity's default. The second promise transfers risk and can have measurable value at inception under IPSAS 41. \[2\]

The correct analogy is not that a guarantee is worthless until called. It is that **exposure, expected loss, collateral and outstanding principal must each be measured separately**.

## 6. A worked sovereign guarantee: four numbers, not one

Imagine a special-purpose company borrows US\$75 million to construct an export-processing facility. Government guarantees a capped US\$20 million of eligible debt-service losses. It receives a hypothetical market-based guarantee fee whose recognised initial value is US\$600,000. For illustration alone, suppose a simplified probability-of-default estimate is 5% and the loss severity on the guaranteed exposure is 40%. The simplified expected loss is US\$20 million × 5% × 40% = US\$400,000, before discounting and without the more complete scenario modelling required by the applicable standard.

The company records the US\$75 million borrowing, not the government. Under an illustrative IPSAS 41 model, government recognises a US\$600,000 initial financial guarantee liability with a corresponding fee consideration, assuming the fee and recognition requirements are satisfied. At a later reporting date, if the unamortised initial amount is US\$540,000 and the properly calculated loss allowance is US\$400,000, the reported guarantee liability is US\$540,000. If credit quality deteriorates and the loss allowance rises to US\$2 million, the liability becomes US\$2 million. In both cases the maximum capped exposure can remain US\$20 million. \[2\]

On a GFSM-style statistical register, the US\$20 million one-off guarantee would ordinarily be reported as a contingent exposure or memorandum item, while the debt remains with the borrower, unless relevant conditions for debt assumption arise. Under an IMF programme, the guarantee could nonetheless count against an applicable programme ceiling. Four figures can therefore coexist: US\$75 million borrower debt; US\$20 million government maximum guaranteed exposure; US\$540,000 illustrative accounting liability; and the programme-defined amount subject to a debt ceiling. \[1\]\[2\]\[4\]

None of these amounts should be quietly substituted for another. The numbers in this example are deliberately hypothetical. Actual entries depend on the contract, fee, initial valuation, probability scenarios, reporting framework and applicable law.

## 7. What happens when a guarantee expires?

Expiry is not an invitation to erase history. A guarantee may expire upon a contractual date, complete repayment of the underlying debt, release by the beneficiary or satisfaction of agreed discharge conditions. Some contracts preserve rights for claims made before the expiry date; others include notice periods, survival clauses, disputed calls or indemnities. Those features must be reviewed before the exposure is marked extinguished.

When all obligations have legally ended and no surviving claim or separate obligation exists, the guarantee should be derecognised according to the relevant accounting standard, while the active guarantee register should be reduced accordingly. Recognised liabilities may first require remeasurement. Historical disclosures, disputed claims and guarantees already called may continue to affect the government's statements. An expired paper is not necessarily a cancelled legal demand, and an undrawn, fully released commitment is not a perpetual liability. \[2\]

Every African treasury should maintain a live digital register linking guarantee number, beneficiary, amount, currency, tenor, trigger, legal authority, project, fee, credit rating, outstanding debt, calls, recoveries, disputes and release evidence. It should be reconciled with lenders, state-owned entities, the debt office, the auditor-general and the accounting function. This is basic sovereign financial management, not a concession to external creditors.

## 8. Why Mozambique must never be reduced to a footnote

Guarantees become genuinely dangerous when the state signs promises that are hidden, unauthorised or economically unserviceable. Mozambique's 2013–14 state-owned-enterprise borrowing crisis is an essential warning. The IMF's 2024 debt-sustainability analysis describes approximately US\$2.2 billion borrowed by EMATUM, MAM and Proindicus and disputes over the legality of associated sovereign guarantees. The episode led to litigation and costly settlements. IMF reporting distinguished disputed contingent claims from debt included in the baseline, showing why legal validity and statistical treatment both matter. \[7\]

This was not a story proving that all guarantees are bad. It was a demonstration that secrecy, governance failure, weak project controls and unclear authority can turn a public promise into a national financial crisis. Nor can Africa solve the problem by relabelling guaranteed borrowing as private finance when the state carries the ultimate losses.

A government that refuses all guarantees may be surrendering opportunity. A government that signs undisclosed guarantees is surrendering the public's balance sheet. The answer is neither prohibition nor recklessness. It is independent scrutiny and enforceable limits.

## 9. Could minerals secure Africa's industrial transformation?

The idea deserves more sophistication than the claim that 'Africa has minerals, therefore Africa has unlimited collateral.' Ore underground is not cash in an escrow account. Mineral deposits require lawful title, competent-person reporting, geological assessment, economic extraction plans, permits, environmental and community obligations, processing capacity, transport access, operating capital, buyers and pricing assumptions. A mineral resource is not automatically a mineral reserve, and neither is automatically a liquid security. CRIRSCO's reporting framework exists precisely to discipline such distinctions. \[11\]

A workable structure starts with a clearly defined project and financing need, not an indefinite pledge of the national mineral estate. Identify a deposit or a measurable royalty or production revenue stream. Commission an independent resource or reserve report and mining plan. Calculate recoverable cash flows under conservative commodity-price and cost scenarios. Establish ownership and the government's legal authority to pledge rights or receivables. Check competing claims, existing offtake and security arrangements, public-debt law, procurement rules, environmental approvals and the rights of local communities.

Next, test the valuation. A competent mineral expert should certify geological estimates; independent engineers should validate production assumptions; financial advisers should model project cash flows and stress scenarios; legal counsel should confirm the security can be perfected and enforced. An actuary may assist with certain stochastic risk modelling, but actuarial certification is not a substitute for geological, engineering, legal or commercial evidence. Treating an indicative 'mineral wealth' figure as immediately pledgeable cash is a serious valuation error.

The security should preferably be tied to ring-fenced, auditable project receivables rather than a blank cheque over sovereign resources. Payment waterfalls can direct export proceeds to operating costs, debt service, reserves, rehabilitation and government revenues. Limit guaranteed amounts and tenor. Specify triggers and cure periods. Prohibit cross-default to unrelated sovereign debts where possible. Publish all material contractual commitments. Obtain parliamentary or other approvals required by local law.

Africa has already used resource-backed financing. The African Development Bank's African Economic Outlook 2025 reports US\$46.5 billion in resource-backed loans across 11 African countries between 2004 and 2018, with Angola accounting for nearly half. Such transactions demonstrate feasibility, not inherent desirability. Commodity-price shocks, opaque contractual terms and creditor seniority can mortgage tomorrow's fiscal room. \[10\]

## 10. A practical alternative: guarantee the gap, not the entire gamble

Suppose an export-oriented copper-processing project costs US\$200 million. This is a constructed scenario, not an identified project or investment forecast. A possible capital stack is US\$60 million sponsor and strategic equity, US\$40 million concessional or development-finance capital, and US\$100 million senior project debt. Before discussing guarantees, prove a reliable power supply, feedstock contracts, operating margins, environmental compliance, technical competence and export offtake.

Instead of guaranteeing all US\$100 million in senior debt, a government could consider a capped US\$25 million guarantee for narrowly defined policy or public-offtaker risks, with appropriate fees, step-downs and termination rights. A development bank or private insurer might cover selected construction, payment or political risks. Lenders should bear genuine credit exposure and sponsors should lose equity first if the venture fails. This would still be a contingent public exposure, and any IMF programme restrictions and national laws would still apply. \[9\]\[15\]

Foreign-exchange earning matters, but it is not an automatic hedge. If US\$80 million of annual export receipts enter the project while US\$50 million must pay for imported feedstock, spare parts and dollar debt service, the gross US\$80 million cannot be treated as free debt-service cover. Test net dollar cash flow, commodity price falls, production disruptions, exchange controls and debt-service reserve adequacy. An export asset that loses money in foreign currency does not become safe merely because it exports.

The public interest requires a plain disclosure: exactly what taxpayers may have to pay under a severe but plausible downside case, and what economic benefit they receive for accepting that risk. If the answer cannot survive parliamentary questioning, it does not belong in a guarantee agreement.

## 11. Africa already has models to examine, not merely slogans to repeat

In March 2026, the World Bank announced support for a South African Credit Guarantee Vehicle designed to attract infrastructure investment using market-based guarantees while reducing dependence on traditional sovereign guarantees. Its planned structure blends public and development-partner capital, charges investors for guarantees and seeks to manage contingent liabilities more sustainably. The initiative is not proof of final commercial outcomes, but it is evidence that guarantee design can evolve beyond Treasury's unlimited signature. \[6\]

The World Bank Group's credit-enhancement products and IFC's partial credit guarantees also demonstrate how credit risk can be allocated rather than simply transferred in full to a government. Historical World Bank reporting about Ghana's Sankofa gas project shows the potential role of risk mitigation in mobilising investment, although each transaction's fiscal exposures and outcomes need their own scrutiny. \[9\]\[14\]\[15\]

The more useful African question is not, 'Why will nobody lend to us?' It is, 'Which risk prevents financing, who can manage it most cheaply, what return does the state receive, and why must taxpayers carry any residual loss?' This reframes development finance from a request for permission into a negotiation about price, risk and productive value.

## 12. An African sovereign guarantee discipline: ten non-negotiable tests

A president asking for a major guarantee should insist on ten conditions before approving it: a publicly identified revenue-producing purpose; a completed independent feasibility study; a named and legally competent issuer; competitive procurement and transparent beneficial ownership; an independently assessed guarantee valuation; a hard liability cap and expiry; a credible sponsor equity contribution; stress-tested project cash flows and currency coverage; a public register and annual legislative disclosure; and an enforceable recovery and release mechanism.

A guarantee should pass two distinct examinations. First is the project examination: would a prudent independent investor finance it, and why? Second is the sovereign examination: can the country honour the guarantee in the downside case without sacrificing essential services? A project can be commercially attractive and still carry an unacceptable sovereign risk. A guarantee can be fiscally affordable and still subsidise a politically connected failure.

Countries should also build domestic competence. Establish a dedicated fiscal-risk unit alongside the public-debt office. Require the auditor-general to examine guarantee disclosures. Use independent investment committees, competitive risk pricing and periodic reporting. Seek technical support from institutions such as the African Legal Support Facility for complex sovereign, infrastructure and extractive transactions, while retaining domestic ownership of decisions. \[12\]

## 13. The president's test: what does your signature actually purchase?

Presidents need not become accountants, geologists or structured-finance lawyers. They must become exacting buyers of their advice. Ask your finance minister to put every active guarantee on one public dashboard. Ask which guarantees built export capacity and which merely postponed insolvency. Ask how many are still legally active despite expired project terms. Ask the IMF country team to explain which restriction is required by the programme and which is a matter of negotiable design. Ask lenders to show their risk margins before and after the guarantee. Ask investors why they want taxpayers' promise while retaining the commercial upside.

Then insist on something seldom found in ceremonial project launches: the counterfactual. What happens if the guarantee is not issued? What other instruments were priced? What would independent project finance cost? Could a political-risk policy, a partial credit guarantee, a dedicated guarantee vehicle, an escrow account or additional sponsor equity deliver the same investment with less sovereign exposure?

If a project cannot answer these questions, the problem is not always the IMF. It may be poor preparation, unreliable numbers or an unwillingness to surrender discretion to a disciplined financing process.

## 14. The real curse is not the guarantee. It is financial illiteracy with sovereign power.

Africa cannot industrialise by refusing every contingent commitment. Nor can it industrialise by treating government guarantees as substitutes for commercially viable projects. A sovereign guarantee is a sophisticated instrument. Used correctly, it can mobilise private finance into power, logistics, manufacturing and exports. Used carelessly, it can convert a ministerial signature into a claim on schools, hospitals and future tax revenues.

What should frighten African leaders is not that someone asks them to measure fiscal risk. It is the possibility that they do not understand the risk they are accepting, the opportunity they are rejecting, or the legal power they are exercising. The strongest answer to external constraint is not denial of accounting reality. It is better project preparation, sharper negotiation, independent verification and a financing architecture that keeps the nation in control.

Africa must stop confusing the right to sign guarantees with the capacity to structure them. The former is a power of office. The latter is a test of leadership.

> A final challenge to every African president: within 100 days, publish your country's sovereign-guarantee register, identify five export-earning projects that deserve disciplined credit enhancement, and show citizens the difference between what is owed, what is guaranteed and what is merely at risk. If those three numbers cannot be explained plainly, they are not yet being governed properly.
