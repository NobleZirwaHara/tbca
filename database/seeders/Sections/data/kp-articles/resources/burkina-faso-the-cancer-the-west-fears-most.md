## The uncomfortable question

Suppose an African country decides that its minerals must be processed at home; that citizens can invest in factories rather than wait for donors; that its agricultural policy must be measured in tonnes harvested and products manufactured; and that a government should own equipment, build roads and bargain harder over the value of its natural resources. Why should any of this be revolutionary?

It should be normal economic policy. Yet Burkina Faso, under Captain Ibrahim Traoré, has made these propositions symbols of an audacious political break. The resulting controversy exposes a deeper African problem: for decades, the continent has discussed development as if the ability to finance imports, negotiate aid and balance budgets were equivalent to the ability to produce. They are not.

The provocation in this article's title is deliberate. I do not mean that Burkina Faso, its people or their aspirations are a disease. The question is whether the *spread of economic self-determination* threatens comfortable assumptions about who should own Africa's resources, choose its partners and determine its industrial future. That is a political and economic question, not evidence that a single, unified “West” is conspiring against the country. Assertions about other governments' secret motives require proof.

Nor is admiration a substitute for audit. An opened factory is observable. A profitable factory is a different proposition. A national refinery is a milestone. It is not, by itself, proof of broad industrialisation. And a government which demands sovereignty from creditors must not be exempt from accountability to its own citizens.

## 1. What has actually changed under Ibrahim Traoré?

Traoré came to power in a military takeover in September 2022 amid a severe insurgency and dissatisfaction with the preceding leadership. His government has subsequently prioritised domestic resource control, agriculture, community shareholding, equipment-led public works and visible manufacturing projects. These are material policy choices, not merely viral slogans. But the source and quality of evidence must accompany every claim.

Consider the facts. The Bobo-Dioulasso tomato-processing plant, SOBTO, was inaugurated in November 2024 at a government-reported construction cost of approximately CFAF 7.5 billion. Burkina Faso's finance ministry described a funding structure of about 80% community equity and 20% state participation. A March 2026 prime-ministerial visit reported that the plant was operating and employed approximately one hundred workers, with additional seasonal and daily employment. The Yako SOFATO tomato plant, inaugurated in December 2024, was reported by the government to have a 100-tonne-per-day *design capacity*, an investment cost of CFAF 5.65 billion, and projected rather than audited employment and turnover. \[4\]\[5\]\[6\]

The distinction matters: design capacity does not equal actual throughput; projected indirect jobs are not measured jobs; a ribbon-cutting is not an audited financial statement. Still, creating a domestic processing enterprise, backed by named equity contributions and physical equipment, is more concrete than issuing another abstract industrial strategy.

As of 30 September 2025, the government reported that its community entrepreneurship agency, APEC, had mobilised approximately CFAF 19.79 billion from 324,301 subscribers, including CFAF 6 billion from the state. The numbers are disclosed by the state; the register of investor rights, audited deployment of funds, profitability and distributions must be independently tested. \[7\]

In agricultural mechanisation, the government announced more than CFAF 104 billion in equipment and inputs for the 2025–26 production campaign. Under Faso Mêbo, it reported distributing 764 items of civil-works equipment in September 2025; in January 2026 the programme was elevated to a public agency. The correct next question is not how many machines appeared in a photograph, but how many hectares were prepared, kilometres were completed, projects passed inspection and machines remained operational after two seasons. \[8\]\[9\]\[10\]

On 28 September 2026, Burkina Faso inaugurated RAFFINOR-BF, a national gold refinery in Ouagadougou. The company and mining ministry reported initial annual refining *capacity* of 164 tonnes, with a modular plan for 515 tonnes. The World Bank reports 94 tonnes of national gold production in 2025. It would therefore be wrong to present 164 tonnes of installed capacity as actual production, or to assume that the value of all refined gold is newly created value. The economic gain must be measured in refining fees retained locally, recovery rates, certification, logistics savings, skilled employment, jewellery manufacture and access to buyers. \[11\]\[12\]\[3\]

These projects are achievements in institution-building and industrial capacity. Whether they become a sustainable transformation depends on performance, security, management and the ability to replicate them without permanent subsidies.

## 2. The numbers refuse both cynicism and propaganda

The World Bank estimated real GDP growth at 5.3% in 2025, compared with 4.8% in 2024. It attributed the improvement to agriculture, more favourable conditions, mining and services, including effects of the government's agricultural offensive and mining reforms. The same institution warned that the economy needs structural changes to turn resilience into productive jobs. \[1\]

The IMF's June 2026 assessment also recorded stronger activity and a 2025 current-account surplus of around 1.1% of GDP. However, the external improvement benefited from gold prices and production, not simply a sudden industrial revolution. In its published table, the IMF put 2025 public debt at roughly 52.1% of GDP, while its growth and 2026 forecasts remained subject to considerable security and commodity-price risks. Estimates and forecasts from different institutions and vintages can differ; they must not be blended into a fictitious single series. \[2\]\[3\]

This is evidence of resilience under difficult conditions. It is not evidence that poverty has disappeared, all imports have been replaced, or Burkina Faso has escaped the global economy. It also does not establish which fraction of recent growth was *caused* by Traoré's initiatives, as opposed to rainfall, gold prices, changes in recorded output or the recovery of production previously disrupted by violence. Attribution requires counterfactual analysis.

A useful test for every president is brutally simple: publish an annual industrial scorecard showing operational factories, installed and actual capacity, net new formal jobs, export receipts from *processed* products, import substitution net of imported inputs, electricity reliability, project returns, fiscal costs and safety outcomes. Publish both the baseline and the methodology. If success is real, it should survive independent measurement.

## 3. The development model Africa ought to interrogate

Much of Africa's policy debate has treated trade liberalisation, privatisation, structural adjustment, aid effectiveness, currency policy and access to multilateral finance as ends in themselves. They are instruments. Their merit depends on whether they increase productivity, competitiveness, human capability and resilience.

Criticism of the inherited model is justified where governments privatised a monopoly without meaningful competition, abandoned manufacturing capabilities, failed to provide electricity and transport, or offered fiscal incentives without enforcing investment commitments. In such circumstances, ownership may have changed while the fundamental production problem remained. Yet the blanket claim that every privatisation destroyed production is indefensible. Different countries and sectors produced different results; regulation, competition, asset management and the institutional environment determine outcomes.

Development aid deserves the same scrutiny. Grants can finance vaccines, emergency food, schools and public health where markets will not. Those are not trivial achievements. But aid that repeatedly finances consumption while leaving local procurement, energy, technical capability and revenue collection unchanged is insufficient as an economic strategy. The 2005 Paris Declaration explicitly endorsed national ownership, alignment and mutual accountability, a useful reminder that development agencies themselves recognised the problem. \[26\]

UN Trade and Development has documented Africa's continued commodity dependence, weak regional connections and exposure to trade shocks. Its 2024 report emphasised infrastructure, higher-value supply chains and intra-African trade. The 2025 commodity-dependence review reinforces the case for value addition rather than reliance on selling unprocessed resources. \[24\]\[25\]

Here is the challenge. If an African government celebrates an aid package worth US\$500 million but cannot identify the productive assets, maintenance arrangements and export capacity it will leave behind, is it financing development or merely financing the next budget cycle? That hypothetical amount is a test of logic, not a claim about any specific country.

## 4. Currency devaluation: a tool, not an industrial policy

Burkina Faso and its neighbours experienced the 1994 devaluation of the CFA franc, an important part of regional economic history. The IMF recorded an immediate surge in prices and a subsequent improvement in price competitiveness and regional economic activity. Later research also found gains after the devaluation, while warning that competitiveness cannot be sustained by the exchange rate alone. \[21\]\[22\]

Two simplistic narratives fail. One says devaluation always makes a country poorer. The other says devaluation automatically creates exporters. Neither is serious economics. It can make imports costlier and reduce consumers' real purchasing power. It can also improve the relative prices of exportable goods. Whether the economy benefits depends on whether farms and factories can actually expand production and whether energy, infrastructure, finance and skills are available.

The lesson of Burkina Faso is not that economics textbooks should be burned. It is that African leaders must stop accepting exchange-rate movements and macroeconomic stability as substitutes for a supply-side programme. A competitive currency without competitive output is a temporary adjustment, not a development model.

## 5. The IMF is not gone. This matters.

One of the most persistent online claims is that Burkina Faso has rejected the IMF and is flourishing without it. The published record contradicts that story. In June 2026 the IMF completed reviews of its Extended Credit Facility and Resilience and Sustainability Facility programmes and approved additional access and disbursements. The IMF reported cumulative ECF disbursements of SDR 180.60 million following the fifth review, with a separate RSF first-review disbursement of SDR 16.42 million. \[2\]

A government can criticise the international financial architecture, negotiate harder and still borrow from the IMF. That is neither an ideological surrender nor proof of independence. The correct issue is whether terms, borrowing limits, domestic reforms and programme spending expand productive capacity at a sustainable cost.

The IMF cannot universally prohibit African countries from issuing sovereign guarantees. Specific IMF-supported programmes may set ceilings or conditions on public and publicly guaranteed debt; domestic law, fiscal risk and creditor appetite also matter. A credit guarantee is not free capital. It shifts a potential default exposure onto taxpayers. The correct reform is to distinguish a disciplined, capped guarantee for an audited income-producing project from an undisclosed political promise to rescue a failing enterprise.

Ask lenders for transparency. Ask governments for disclosure of every guarantee, its maximum exposure, expected loss, fees, collateral, project cash flows and approval authority. If a financing mechanism cannot survive that test, calling it “sovereign” will not make it sound.

## 6. The real innovation is ownership attached to production

Burkina Faso's most interesting experiment is not anti-Western rhetoric. It is an attempt to link domestic savings, state participation and production facilities. The tomato plants are intelligible examples: citizens subscribe capital, machinery is installed, local produce is processed, and a product appears on the market. \[4\]\[5\]\[7\]

But community shareholding can fail if it becomes compulsory patriotism disguised as an investment. Every subscriber must know whether the instrument is ordinary equity, a cooperative share, a grant or a non-refundable contribution. Investors need prospectuses, audited accounts, anti-dilution rules, shareholder registers, procurement oversight, dividends policy, grievance channels and the right to inspect performance.

The strongest version of this model would finance a commercially credible value chain: reliable irrigation; farmer aggregation; cold storage; quality inspection; packaging; road access; electricity; commercial offtake; and traceable payments to producers. A tomato factory without contracted tomato supply may sit idle. A mine without secure transport can close. A refinery without certification and customers cannot create industrial depth from symbolism.

The question is not whether the state or private sector should prevail everywhere. It is whether the financing and ownership structure is matched to the risk, capabilities and market of each sector. Competitive private enterprise, cooperatives, state development institutions and foreign investors can coexist under clear rules.

## 7. Machines are good. Maintenance is sovereignty.

Images of tractors, graders and graders' depots have become potent symbols of new state capacity. The symbolism has substance when underinvestment in equipment is genuinely holding back farming, feeder roads and urban works. Burkina Faso's public announcements on the agricultural offensive and Faso Mêbo show a deliberate mobilisation of productive tools. \[8\]\[9\]

However, real mechanisation is an economic system, not a procurement event. Each machine needs trained operators, spare parts, fuel, servicing, utilisation targets, insurance where relevant, digital maintenance logs and an accountable beneficiary. Before celebrating a fleet of 764 units, publish average utilisation hours, downtime, project cost per kilometre, asset losses and the share of repairs performed by local technicians. The 764 is a reported equipment count, not an audited increase in output.

Africa should build domestic capacity for parts, implements, pumps, road surfacing, repair workshops, industrial controls and fabrication. The machine that must return overseas for every part is less sovereign than the machine that can be maintained locally. This is where procurement policy becomes industrial policy.

## 8. Gold: owning the ore is not the same as owning the value chain

The 2024 Burkina Faso Mining Code increased the state's free-carried participation in mining ventures from 10% to 15% according to IMF analysis, and reworked royalty terms. The state also transferred selected acquired mining assets to the state participation company SOPAMIB in 2025. These steps change the formal bargaining position of the government. \[13\]\[14\]

The national refinery then raises a vital question: how much value can be retained from gold beyond extraction? The answers include verified local refining, credible assaying, secure vaulting, accredited bullion sales, jewellery making, skilled metallurgy, data systems, local contractors and transparent tax and royalty collection. Some steps create more value than others. Refining bullion to higher purity can improve control and fees but does not magically reproduce the full market value of the gold as new national income.

The fiscal arithmetic must be published. Suppose, purely as an illustration, a plant processes 100 tonnes of gold and retains a net processing margin of US\$0.10 per gram. Because 100 metric tonnes equal 100 million grams, the *illustrative* gross processing margin is US\$10 million before plant finance, security, energy, inputs, depreciation and tax. But US\$0.10 per gram is an assumption, not an observed Burkina Faso fee; a modest change in the assumption radically alters the result. The point of this thought experiment is to demand real audited margins rather than copy viral claims about billions of “new” wealth.

A better test is the difference between the local value retained under the new arrangement and what would have been retained under an otherwise comparable arrangement, after all economic costs and risk. This counterfactual will not fit on a billboard. It is what responsible economic leadership requires.

## 9. The Sahel alliance: bold politics, difficult economics

Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger formed the Alliance of Sahel States and subsequently built a confederal political framework. Their withdrawal from ECOWAS became effective on 29 January 2025. ECOWAS, while acknowledging the withdrawal, urged continuity of passport recognition, movement rights and trade preferences pending negotiations. \[15\]

The regional bloc has two distinct opportunities. First, it can collaborate more effectively against armed violence and build bargaining strength in external relations. Second, it can create larger markets for agricultural processing, mining services, energy, logistics and technical training. A cross-border industrial cluster may be more competitive than three small, isolated national plants making the same product.

Yet geography cannot be negotiated away. All three AES states are landlocked. They need functioning trade corridors through coastal neighbours, safe roads, interoperable customs, affordable freight and trusted payment channels. Treating ECOWAS states as permanent enemies would undermine the very commercial sovereignty the AES seeks to gain. Political autonomy and economic interdependence are not opposites.

Regional sovereignty should be measured through reductions in border delays, freight cost per container, interruption days, qualified suppliers and increased intra-regional manufacturing trade. A new flag without cheaper transport is not industrial integration.

## 10. Have Burkina Faso and the Sahel abandoned the dollar and joined BRICS?

No. Claims that the AES has already introduced a common currency replacing the CFA franc have circulated widely and have been fact-checked as false. The IMF's 2026 Burkina Faso country materials continue to identify the CFA franc as legal tender in the West African monetary union, pegged at CFAF 655.957 per euro. Burkina Faso is not listed among the BRICS full members in the South African government's September 2026 summary. \[16\]\[17\]\[23\]

This does not mean alternatives are impossible. African governments can diversify their trade partners, negotiate settlement arrangements, deepen African capital markets, improve regional payments and seek investment from Gulf, Asian, European, American and African institutions. The issue is leverage, not replacing one dependency with another.

BRICS should be evaluated as a set of possible trade and investment relationships, not worshipped as a rescue mechanism. A Russian or Chinese lender can impose hard commercial terms. A European or American manufacturer can bring valuable technology. The nationality of the investor is less important than the contract: local content, technology transfer, FX exposure, offtake, fair taxation, dispute resolution and retention of productive capabilities.

## 11. What does “the West fears” actually mean?

“The West” is not a single actor with one balance sheet, strategy or emotion. Governments, lenders, manufacturers, mining houses, pension funds and consumers have differing interests. A producer that builds its own processing plant may reduce margins for an offshore processor; it may also create opportunities for suppliers of machinery, certification, electricity and equipment. Outcomes are sector-specific.

The more defensible proposition is this: any incumbent commercial arrangement can be challenged when a resource-rich producer demands a larger share of value, more local employment and alternative suppliers. The threat is to an *advantageous contract or business model*, not proof of a clandestine campaign by all Western countries.

Africa should stop asking whether foreigners approve of its industrialisation. It should ask whether its own policies can survive competition, a change in political leadership and independent scrutiny. Manufacturing strength is more difficult to dismiss than an inflammatory speech. It is also much harder to build.

## 12. Religion, ideology and the economics of responsibility

The sketch behind this article asks whether passive religious teachings have weakened productivity. That is a legitimate subject for social debate, but there is no credible basis here to blame Africa's industrial difficulties on a particular faith or to claim the Traoré government has implemented a clearly documented anti-religious productivity reform.

Faith can motivate work, mutual aid, education, enterprise and civic duty. It can also be invoked, like any ideology, to justify passivity or to excuse leaders from accountability. What matters for economic policy is the public bargain: schools that teach usable skills; laws that protect workers and property; public officials who deliver services; and cultures that reward creation rather than political proximity.

No sermon can replace stable power at the factory gate. No slogan can replace a machinist. No military parade can replace a payroll. And no political leader should ask citizens to sacrifice accountability in exchange for the promise of production.

## 13. The price Burkina Faso still pays

There is a second Burkina Faso that rarely appears in celebratory videos: displaced families, closed classrooms, besieged settlements and civilians exposed to violence. UNICEF estimates that 4.5 million people, including 2.9 million children, require humanitarian assistance in 2026. \[18\]

Human Rights Watch's April 2026 investigation documented more than 1,800 civilian deaths in incidents involving the military and allied militias as well as an al-Qaeda-linked armed group since 2023, and reported grave abuses by multiple parties. These are the organisation's documented findings, not a judicial conviction of any identified person. They must be addressed through credible investigation, civilian protection and accountability. \[19\]

In January 2026 the authorities also dissolved political parties, an extraordinary restriction on pluralist politics. A government can purchase machines while weakening mechanisms that expose corruption, poor procurement or civilian abuse. Those two realities can coexist. \[20\]

Industrial transformation is harder during insecurity. Displaced farmers cannot cultivate predictable harvests; transporters face interrupted routes; insurers increase premiums; teachers leave; workers cannot travel. Therefore, civilian protection, justice, a lawful return to political competition and a functioning independent media are not luxuries to be postponed until after economic development. They are conditions that help sustain it.

A nation is not sovereign merely because its government defies foreign diplomats. Citizens must also be secure against arbitrary power at home.

## 14. The five tests of a genuine African industrial revolution

**Test one: additional production.** Count what is produced beyond the pre-project baseline, not the number of factories launched. Separate domestic sales from exports and measure quality and unit costs.

**Test two: genuine economic value.** Report value added after imported equipment, imported intermediate goods, logistics, operating costs and the opportunity cost of public capital. Use independent audits and published methodologies.

**Test three: employment and skills.** Count sustained direct and indirect jobs without double-counting; publish wage ranges, safety outcomes and the number of local technical workers trained to operate and repair capital assets.

**Test four: fiscal and financial sustainability.** Disclose total capital spent, sources of funds, debt-service needs, state guarantees, dividends, subsidies, maintenance backlogs and contingent liabilities. An enterprise that indefinitely consumes the school budget is not self-reliance.

**Test five: legitimate institutions.** Protect shareholder rights, fair procurement, property rights, civilian security, contestable elections and independent reporting. Economic sovereignty without public accountability can become a new domestic form of extraction.

On these tests, Burkina Faso has visible, noteworthy initiatives but insufficient published independent operating data to declare a completed industrial transformation. This is not an insult. It is the performance standard every African government deserves.

## 15. A practical blueprint for presidents who want results, not applause

**First 100 days: diagnose the productive economy.** Commission a factory-by-factory inventory, an agricultural supply map, a mineral value-chain analysis, an electricity reliability audit, a skills census and a public register of industrial incentives. Identify the ten largest import categories that local producers could realistically supply at competitive prices. Release the baseline figures and procurement data.

**First year: launch a small number of bankable clusters.** Select three to five value chains based on domestic input supply, reliable power, identified customers and export potential. Examples may include tomato processing, edible oils, cotton spinning, fertiliser blending, equipment maintenance or mineral services. These are candidate sectors, not a universal prescription. Require feasibility studies, life-cycle costs, buyer commitments, environmental approvals and competitive procurement.

**Years two to three: establish project-finance discipline.** Use blended finance only where justified. Structure investments with disclosed government support, capped guarantees, independent board appointments and ring-fenced revenues. If a processing hub requires a hypothetical US\$50 million, its financing could be US\$20 million equity and US\$30 million debt. That is a funding illustration, not a market quote or a prediction. Investors must stress-test prices, volumes, utilisation, foreign-exchange movements and security interruptions before capital is committed.

**Years three to five: connect regional markets.** Negotiate cross-border transport and customs reforms, standards recognition, technical training and export offtake through both AES and neighbouring ECOWAS economies. Build capacity to manufacture and maintain equipment. Set published targets for domestic value added and employment and permit independent comparisons with the baseline.

**Every year: permit reality to contradict the leader.** Publish annual audited scorecards. Shut failing ventures or restructure them. Reward commercially effective managers regardless of party or personal loyalty. Support affected workers and shareholders transparently. A government that cannot admit an unsuccessful project has surrendered to its own propaganda.

## 16. The question to Africa's presidents

Are you asking for a better loan, or building a productive economy that needs fewer rescue loans? Are you announcing another factory, or publishing its electricity uptime, capacity utilisation, sales and profit? Are you bargaining for more mining royalties, or building local capabilities to drill, assay, refine, fabricate and market? Are you changing alliances because the contract improves, or because the flag on the lender's building has changed?

Traoré's Burkina Faso should challenge African leaders precisely because some of its actions are concrete. Ordinary people can see equipment, factories and an attempt to make economic participation more local. Those achievements deserve acknowledgement. The record also shows unfinished economics, dependence on IMF financing, severe security costs and serious concerns about the treatment of civilians and political rights. Those facts demand candour, not silence.

The lesson worth exporting is not military rule, hero worship, confrontation for its own sake or a false story of total financial liberation. It is the insistence that African states should own a greater share of their productive future, mobilise domestic capital, build viable industries and demand contracts that reward local capability. And it is the equally important insistence that results be verified and citizens protected.

**Africa's real revolution will begin when a president's greatest achievement is not the speech that excites the crowd, but the enterprise that continues producing, paying taxes, training workers and exporting long after that president has left office.**

If that idea alarms an entrenched system, the answer is not to demand permission. It is to build, measure and govern well enough that the results cannot be denied.
