There are discoveries that change what we know. Others change what we are prepared to question. Africa's distorted place on the familiar world map belongs to the second category. The scandal is not that cartography involves choices. Every flat map must distort a spherical world. The uncomfortable question is why one particular distortion became so routine that generations could mistake an unsuitable comparison of continental areas for an objective picture of the planet.

On 4 September 2026, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 80/307, **“Correct the map”**, by 164 votes in favour, one against and six abstentions. The resolution promotes more accurate cartographic representations of continental areas, particularly Africa. This was an African-led initiative, advanced by Togo with African Union support. It is an invitation to change institutional practice, not an order prohibiting existing navigation maps or rewriting the world's geography. \[1\]\[2\]\[3\]

Twenty days later, at the United Nations, Barbados Prime Minister Mia Amor Mottley challenged the thinking that allows an inaccurate representation to survive. She declared: **“It is the same mindset that caused a global map to remain grossly inaccurate on the size of Africa for centuries.”** Her intervention linked cartographic representation to longstanding inequities in global institutions. It did not constitute proof that international economic databases had been deliberately falsified. It did something more useful: it demanded scrutiny of the assumptions that powerful institutions ask others to accept. \[4\]

Africa should accept that invitation, but with intellectual discipline. If we respond to one misleading picture by treating every statistic as fraudulent, we will exchange a bad map for bad reasoning. If we respond by independently measuring our people, land, production, trade, skills, assets and opportunities, we may finally give governments the evidence required to govern rather than guess.

## 1. WHAT EXACTLY WAS WRONG WITH THE MAP?

The Mercator projection, developed in 1569, was designed to preserve local angles and compass bearings, valuable characteristics for navigation. It does not preserve relative area. Places near the poles are shown disproportionately large relative to equatorial regions. Africa did not physically shrink. Greenland did not physically expand. Their shapes were converted to a flat plane using mathematics that serves one purpose well and another badly. Equal-area projections, including Equal Earth, are better suited to comparing continental sizes, although they too make different compromises in shape and direction. \[1\]\[3\]

A responsible critique must separate **technical origin**, **historical use** and **political effect**. There is no reliable evidence in the UN resolution that the original Mercator projection was engineered as a secret plan to conceal Africa's landmass. Its widespread use in atlases, classrooms and public presentations nonetheless made its misleading area comparison appear normal. In societies where visual size can become associated with economic or geopolitical importance, that repeated image can reinforce an unequal impression. That is the interpretation promoted by the African Union's Correct the Map initiative. \[2\]\[3\]

Some critics may dismiss the campaign as symbolism. But symbols shape public understanding, especially when they are mistaken for scientific neutrality. Conversely, no map projection alone explains Africa's colonial history, trade structure, financing costs or policy failures. The test of intellectual seriousness is whether the campaign becomes the starting point for better measurement, or simply another slogan printed on a better map.

## 2. THE VOTE WAS REAL. THE REFORM IS NOT AUTOMATIC.

The UN vote was substantial: Resolution A/RES/80/307 passed on 4 September 2026 by **164 to one, with six abstentions**. The text encourages more accurate equal-area cartographic representations, supports national geospatial capability and asks for implementation information to be reported through the UN system. It does **not** legally require every school, software provider or government to replace a map overnight. The distinction matters. An adopted international resolution creates a powerful political standard; actual change still requires new textbooks, procurement specifications, digital designs, teacher training and institutional follow-through. \[1\]\[2\]

African presidents should therefore avoid celebrating a diplomatic success as though it had already rebuilt national capability. One can win a vote on a world map while remaining unable to produce an updated village map, an agricultural cadastre, a reliable business register or a trustworthy inventory of public land. That contradiction is the real subject of this article.

## 3. DOES A DISTORTED MAP MAKE IMF OR WORLD BANK DATA FALSE?

No. Not by itself. Cartographic distortion, census errors, data estimation, forecasting bias, omitted economic activity and intentional manipulation are different phenomena. A mathematical projection chosen for navigation is not evidence that a country's poverty figures, exports or credit rating were deliberately doctored. Serious accusations require specific datasets, documented methods, audit trails, identified discrepancies and evidence of who changed what, when and why.

Equally, it would be naive to treat all published numbers as immaculate. Measurement is difficult. Some national statistics are built from old business registers, infrequent household surveys, estimates of activity that authorities cannot observe directly, and administrative systems that do not communicate. International databases may make further conversions, interpolations or harmonisation adjustments. The reliability of a statistic depends on its source, sampling, coverage, methodology, revision history, timeliness and disclosed uncertainty. \[5\]\[6\]

Here is the institutional distinction leaders must understand. **National statistical offices and other domestic agencies generally originate much of the underlying official data.** The World Bank and IMF are major users, compilers, publishers and providers of technical assistance; they are not, as a general rule, the proprietors of every country's census or the sole controllers of its raw data. The World Bank's published statistical principles explicitly recognise country ownership. The IMF's data-dissemination initiatives encourage national authorities to publish their own macroeconomic series. An argument for African sovereignty therefore begins by strengthening African producers of statistics, not by falsely claiming that all numbers were invented abroad. \[6\]\[7\]\[8\]

The right question is sharper: **When figures from a national ministry, a customs administration, a company, a satellite and an international database disagree, who in Africa can reconcile the evidence, explain the difference and publish a defensible result?** That is a sovereignty question with budgetary consequences.

## 4. THE EVIDENCE THAT SHOULD ALARM EVERY FINANCE MINISTER

Consider Nigeria. In 2014, its National Bureau of Statistics released rebased GDP estimates using a much newer economic structure. An IMF account reported that the estimated nominal GDP for **2013 increased from US\$286.5 billion to US\$521.8 billion**, an increase of approximately **82.2%**. Calculation: (521.8 − 286.5) ÷ 286.5 × 100 = 82.1%, with the published table reporting 82.2% because of rounding of the underlying figures. This was not a sudden discovery of new oil wells or an overnight windfall. The statistical system had caught up with parts of the economy that earlier estimates inadequately represented. \[9\]

Ghana supplies an earlier lesson. In 2010, the Ghana Statistical Service updated methods and sources and revised the estimate of **2006 GDP upward by about 60%**. Once again, new measurement did not put 60% more money into people's pockets. It changed the denominator used to understand national output, productivity, debt ratios, tax performance and economic structure. \[10\]

| **Case**      | **Documented revision**                            | **What it does not mean**             |
|---------------|----------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Nigeria, 2014 | 2013 nominal GDP: US\$286.5bn to US\$521.8bn       | Not 82% new economic output overnight |
| Ghana, 2010   | 2006 GDP estimate revised upward approximately 60% | Not 60% new household income          |

These are **documented revisions**, not evidence that the IMF or World Bank had secretly suppressed African wealth. Nigeria's rebasing involved technical collaboration with international development institutions. The irony is more instructive: statistical weaknesses can survive for years in plain sight, and revisions can radically alter the way an economy is described. A government that cannot explain the age of its production weights should not be surprised when lenders, investors and citizens question its numbers. \[9\]\[10\]

The uncomfortable leadership question follows. How many new enterprises, informal supply chains, logistics operators, digital platforms, irrigated farms, industrial workshops and cross-border transactions are inadequately represented in today's national data? There is no credible continent-wide answer to that question. That uncertainty is itself a policy problem, not permission to invent one.

## 5. POVERTY: DO WE COUNT PEOPLE, OR DO WE COUNT ASSUMPTIONS?

Poverty statistics deserve scrutiny precisely because public policy depends on them. A poverty estimate rests on choices about who is surveyed, how consumption or income is measured, how prices differ across locations, how households are weighted and how poverty thresholds are defined. International poverty measures also use purchasing-power-parity conversions to improve comparability. A person may appear above one threshold and below another without anything changing in that person's life. This is a methodological distinction, not proof of a plot.

A national audit should ask whether remote settlements, informal dwellings, migrants, displaced households and fast-growing towns are adequately covered. It should test the cost of living in places where a national price index may conceal large regional differences. It should publish confidence intervals, survey dates, nonresponse rates, revisions and definitions. A policy cannot be called evidence-based if ministers repeat a poverty percentage but cannot say which survey produced it.

The same discipline must extend to unemployment. An official unemployment rate is not identical to the number of adults without secure livelihoods; definitions distinguish those seeking work, discouraged jobseekers, unpaid family work and informal activity. Governments need several complementary measures: employment, underemployment, hours, earnings, skills utilisation, transitions into formal work and youth labour-market exclusion. They need these measures at district level, not only in a single national headline.

## 6. TRADE: DOES THE CONTAINER MATCH THE DECLARATION?

Africa's trade data must be tested against the movement of actual goods. A customs declaration, a bill of lading, a border scan, a warehouse receipt, a mining production ledger, a foreign-exchange settlement and a partner country's import record can describe different stages of the same transaction. Differences may be legitimate: export values are commonly recorded on a different valuation basis from imports; goods may transit through third countries; classification, recording dates and exchange rates may differ. Other discrepancies may indicate misclassification, under-invoicing, smuggling or gaps in administration. The correct response is reconciliation, not assumption. \[11\]

Governments should build product-level, corridor-level trade accounts that reconcile quantities and values across mining ministries, customs, ports, rail systems, tax agencies and central banks. They should publish discrepancies with explanations rather than bury them. Revenue services should link major extractive shipments to independent quantity assays and export records. The EITI Standard explicitly calls for the disclosure of extractive production and export quantities and values, with methods sufficiently clear for public oversight. \[11\]

A country that knows the declared value of its copper exports but cannot verify shipment weights, mineral grades or the beneficial owners of trading intermediaries is operating with a strategic blind spot. The potential fiscal consequences are real; their size must be measured transaction by transaction, not asserted without evidence.

## 7. THE WEALTH BELOW THE SOIL IS NOT A BANK BALANCE

Your geological potential is not the same thing as a proved mineral reserve. Geological occurrence, inferred mineral resource, measured resource and economically recoverable reserve are not interchangeable. Competent geological work must establish quantity, grade, location, recovery assumptions, environmental and legal constraints, infrastructure requirements and commercial viability. The US Geological Survey distinguishes the broader concept of mineral resources from reserves that are economically mineable under relevant conditions. \[12\]

A drone cannot prove the tonnage of gold beneath a mountain. A satellite can reveal geological and land-cover clues, not substitute for drilling, laboratory assays, metallurgical testing and qualified reporting. Nor does valuing a mineral deposit create legally enforceable collateral. Investors must still assess title, licensing, lender security, community rights, environmental permissions, production costs and market demand.

This is where Africa needs an independently tested **National Strategic Resource Register**: known deposits and their classifications; exploration licences; reserves reported to recognised technical standards; concession ownership; production; royalties; export quantities; environmental liabilities; associated power, water, road and rail constraints. Public summaries should be accessible. Commercially sensitive and protected information should be handled under law. Estimates must carry dates and uncertainty ranges. A credible register can support better investment decisions without pretending that underground potential is cash in the treasury.

## 8. ARABLE LAND: A GREEN PATCH IS NOT AN AGRICULTURAL BUSINESS

The claim that Africa has vast agricultural potential becomes operational only when potential is separated from **currently cultivated**, **potentially suitable**, **irrigable**, **legally available**, **environmentally sustainable** and **economically accessible** land. Soil fertility, slope, water availability, distance to roads and markets, tenure rights, biodiversity and climate exposure determine whether an apparent opportunity can support profitable production. \[13\]

FAO's World Programme for the Census of Agriculture identifies land tenure, land use, holdings, irrigation, livestock, labour and inputs as core information for national agricultural planning. Satellite imagery helps map land cover and changes over time; household and farm surveys, soil testing and ground visits establish the facts that pixels cannot. \[13\]

Imagine a government announcing one million hectares of new agricultural land. Without district-level evidence of water rights, soils, current occupants, infrastructure and production economics, that number is a political aspiration rather than an investable agricultural pipeline. An honest map should show, parcel by parcel, what is possible, what is prohibited, what infrastructure is missing and whose rights are affected.

## 9. SKILLS, FIRMS, ENERGY AND ROADS: THE ECONOMY NOBODY CAN SEE

A state that wants to industrialise must know more than its mineral endowment. Where are functioning workshops? How many firms produce machine parts, fertiliser, packaging, food-processing equipment or electrical components? What is their spare capacity? Which municipalities lose production hours to power interruptions? Which district roads make otherwise viable farms uncompetitive? Which technical skills are genuinely scarce, in what quantities and at what wages?

An updated economic census should connect establishment registers, tax records, utility demand, manufacturing surveys and business associations. A skills observatory should link qualifications, vacancies, wages, employer surveys, apprenticeships and actual job placements. The resulting picture must capture informal enterprises without forcing disclosure that endangers vulnerable workers or violates confidentiality. Even excellent AI models cannot fill a register that systematically excludes the enterprises one is trying to support.

Credit assessments should also be examined more carefully. Sovereign ratings and lending decisions are judgments based on numerous factors, including fiscal capacity and institutional risk; they do not mechanically equal the 'true value' of a country. Better resource and enterprise statistics may improve investor due diligence, but cannot alone erase debt-service pressure, weak legal enforcement or governance risk. Evidence should inform financing, not be mistaken for financing.

## 10. WHAT AI, AGENTIC AI, RPA AND DRONES CAN ACTUALLY DO

Technology can fundamentally improve the **speed, geographical coverage and consistency** of public measurement. But the words 'artificial intelligence' must not become a new justification for replacing evidence with attractive estimates. The UN's Global Statistical Geospatial Framework, updated in its second edition and endorsed through UN statistical processes in 2025–2026, provides a credible approach to integrating location data with official statistics. \[14\]

**Earth observation and drones:** Satellite images can detect changes in buildings, vegetation, cultivation and infrastructure corridors. Approved drone surveys can offer finer local resolution for cadastral, agricultural or public-works work. Output must be validated in the field and comply with aviation, privacy and land-access law. A detected roof is not automatically an occupied household; a detected field is not automatically a legally available farm.

**AI and machine learning:** Properly validated models can classify land cover, identify unusual trade declarations, flag duplicate business records, forecast areas requiring field inspection and detect inconsistencies between administrative databases. Every material model needs a documented training dataset, error rate, sample validation, drift monitoring and an appeal or correction mechanism.

**Agentic AI:** Governed software agents can collect reports from ministries, cross-check public datasets, request explanations for anomalies, compile proposed revisions and produce traceable evidence packs for statistical officers. Their role is to prepare and test, not to certify national facts without accountable human sign-off. The audit log must record each source, query, transformation, exception and approval.

**Robotic process automation:** RPA can transfer data from older administrative systems, validate routine fields, match identifiers and prepare monthly reconciliations. It will repeat errors reliably if the underlying rules are wrong. Accordingly, the first investment is in data definitions and stewardship, not bots.

**Human specialists:** Enumerators, geologists, surveyors, auditors, statisticians, hydrologists, land-rights experts and affected communities remain indispensable. High-quality statistical systems combine field evidence, remote sensing and transparent professional judgment. Technology should expand their reach, not erase their accountability. \[13\]\[14\]

## 11. HOW AFRICA CAN CONTROL ITS DATA WITHOUT HIDING IT

There is a difference between **data sovereignty** and **data secrecy**. A sovereign statistical system makes African public institutions capable of gathering, validating, securing and explaining their own evidence. It does not mean that political leaders own the numbers or may alter them. Public confidence requires independent national statistics offices, published methods, predictable release calendars, protection of personal information, independent audits and open access to suitable aggregated datasets. These are not foreign impositions. They are principles formally endorsed by the United Nations General Assembly and adopted in national statistical practice, including by Statistics South Africa. \[5\]

Presidents should sign a **Statistical Independence Compact**: no political approval of numerical results; transparent appointment of heads of statistics; adequate recurring budgets; published methodological changes; external peer review; and legal protection for confidential microdata. Citizens must be able to challenge mistakes and obtain corrections without needing access to identifiable records. Sovereignty resides in institutional capability and public accountability, not in one official's authority to declare a number true.

Africa already has institutions that can help. The African Development Bank's **Africa Information Highway** connects country data portals and regional institutions, while the African Union Commission's **Trade Observatory Unit** supports harmonised trade evidence. Afreximbank's **Africa Trade Gateway**, including its MANSA due-diligence platform, offers complementary business and trade intelligence. These are distinct initiatives: there is no verified basis for calling them a single official 'Afreximbank Bureau' certifying every African census or resource valuation. Their appropriate role is partnership and cross-checking, not replacing independent national statistical authorities. \[15\]\[16\]\[17\]

## 12. A CONTINENTAL DATA ASSURANCE SYSTEM: FIVE TESTS

Every strategic dataset submitted to a cabinet, investor, development bank or public portal should pass five tests before it is labelled dependable.

**Provenance:** Who collected it? For what purpose? Who supplied source records? Which geographic areas and periods does it cover? Where are the original evidence files and lawful access permissions?

**Method:** What definition, classification, sampling design, model, valuation basis and calculation method were used? Are the units and currencies explicit? Was the measure observed directly, estimated or forecast?

**Reconciliation:** Does the dataset agree with independent sources such as censuses, utility meters, port movements, partner-country trade data, physical inspections and public registers? Where it does not agree, is there a documented explanation?

**Uncertainty:** What is the confidence interval, quality rating, missing-data share, geological classification or forecast range? How old is the observation? Has the number been independently reviewed?

**Publication and correction:** Is there a stable version number, responsible officer, public metadata, correction procedure, complaints channel, privacy protection and retained revision history?

These standards matter more than the nationality of the server hosting a spreadsheet. A locally hosted but politically manipulated dataset is not sovereign evidence. A shared, professionally audited dataset produced under African authority can be both internationally comparable and firmly accountable to African citizens.

## 13. THE PRESIDENTIAL ASSIGNMENT: EIGHT REGISTERS WORTH BUILDING

Every African government should commission a gap assessment of its current information systems before ordering expensive new surveys. It should then prioritise eight linked registers, administered by legally responsible agencies and published at an appropriate level of detail.

**1. Population and address register:** A sound population and housing census, supported between censuses by civil registration and carefully governed administrative updates. Existing census results should be reviewed before costly duplication. The UN's census programmes favour regular, comparable measurement, not perpetual ad hoc re-counting. \[18\]

**2. Land and land-rights register:** Surveyed parcel boundaries, tenure categories, lawful occupiers, land-use restrictions, disputes and environmental protections, with safeguards against dispossession and false certainty.

**3. Agriculture and water register:** Farm holdings, actual cropping, irrigation, soil capability, watershed limits, storage and route-to-market constraints, aligned with FAO's 2030 agricultural census guidance. \[13\]

**4. Geological and extractive register:** Mineral occurrences and professionally classified resources or reserves, licences, ownership, production and export data, royalties and closure obligations, checked against EITI disclosure requirements. \[11\]\[12\]

**5. Enterprise and industrial-capacity register:** Firm locations, sector classifications, investment needs, production capacity, supplier relationships and domestic value-add opportunities, protecting commercially sensitive data.

**6. Workforce and skills register:** Occupation demand, competency assessments, apprenticeships, qualification outcomes and placement results, disaggregated geographically and refreshed with employer evidence.

**7. Infrastructure-service register:** Power reliability, generation and connections, water availability, roads, rail, ports, broadband and logistics bottlenecks, mapped to production zones.

**8. Trade and investment register:** Commodity flows, verified project pipeline, permitting status, direct investment stocks and flows, foreign-exchange implications and project-level investment readiness.

None of these should become eight disconnected political dashboards. Shared identifiers, geographical standards, secure interoperable systems and independent statistical leadership should make them mutually intelligible. A mineral concession, its power substation, access railway, skills catchment and export terminal should ultimately be analysable together.

## 14. THE 100-DAY, ONE-YEAR AND THREE-YEAR TEST

**In the first 100 days**, each president should publish an inventory of national datasets, their owners, dates, coverage, known gaps and public access rules. The finance minister and national statistician should identify ten economically important decisions currently being made with inadequate information. Cabinet should adopt a funded data-improvement mandate, protect statistical independence and begin a public revision register. These are proposals, not claims about existing government commitments.

**Within one year**, launch priority field validation in a limited number of production corridors and municipalities. Integrate at least three high-impact datasets, such as enterprise locations, power reliability and transport access. Publish a reconciled extractive production-export pilot and a district-level agricultural suitability pilot. Commission independent peer reviews before presenting the outputs as investment-grade. Set service standards for correction requests and publication delays. The purpose of a pilot is to discover error before national scale magnifies it.

**Within three years**, subject to capacity and funding, build a national statistical-geospatial platform conforming to international interoperability and confidentiality standards. Schedule periodic economic and agricultural censuses appropriately. Publish resource-data quality ratings and investment-pipeline evidence packs. Enable universities, firms, legislatures and communities to contest official interpretations with access to non-sensitive methods and data. Evaluate success by improved coverage, lower revision delays, documented corrections, independently validated accuracy and more informed project selection, not by the number of drones purchased.

The realistic financing question is not, 'How many billions can we put on a digital platform slide?' It is, 'Which inaccurate decisions are we currently paying for, and what would credible measurement allow us to stop wasting?' Each investment should have a costed business case, information-security assessment, open procurement and measurable public benefit.

## 15. A WARNING: THE NEW MAP CAN ALSO BE MISUSED

A campaign against distorted representation can itself become distorted if it declares that all unfavourable data is colonial propaganda. Africa's statistical record includes capable national agencies, internationally comparable datasets, independent research and demonstrable methodological corrections. To reject these wholesale would undermine African experts whose work makes reform possible. National pride must not become a licence to suppress unemployment figures, conceal debt, inflate mineral reserves or silence a census that produces inconvenient results.

There are at least four possible sources of a disputed statistic: legitimate methodological differences, missing information, poor measurement and deliberate manipulation. These require different responses. Publish methods for the first; collect more evidence for the second; correct systems for the third; investigate and prosecute proven misconduct for the fourth. Accusation is not an audit. Patriotism is not a methodology. A model's confidence score is not proof.

International institutions should be subjected to transparent methodological scrutiny, just as African governments should. External financing, expertise or software does not automatically compromise results; nor does African ownership automatically make results accurate. Confidence is earned through demonstrable procedures, adversarial checks, independent review and public accountability. That is the standard worth demanding from every side.

## 16. PRESIDENTS OF AFRICA: WHAT ARE YOU GOVERNING IF YOU CANNOT MEASURE IT?

Mr President, can your administration state, with evidence, how many potentially investable agricultural hectares are legally and sustainably available? Can it distinguish an inferred mineral occurrence from a bankable reserve? Can it identify its five most important manufacturing supply-chain gaps by district? Can it reconcile the weight of mineral exports recorded by customs with production declared by mines? Can it describe the last major GDP revision and what changed? Can it publish the reliability of electricity supply to strategic industrial sites? Can it tell young people which occupations are expanding, and show the employer evidence behind that claim?

If the answer is no, then the challenge is not merely that others once made Africa look smaller on a world map. The deeper challenge is that governments may be asking lenders to finance projects whose inputs they have not adequately measured, allocating public money without sufficient local evidence, and announcing national development plans without reliable production baselines.

Stop confusing a development vision with a dataset. Stop praising a resource estimate that nobody has drilled, a factory pipeline that nobody has audited, or a census that nobody can explain. Give your statisticians independence. Give your geological survey proper instruments. Give your municipalities interoperable registers. Give your citizens the right to verify non-sensitive public evidence. Give entrepreneurs the data needed to invest and the certainty needed to plan.

There is no dignity in replacing a distorted map with fabricated statistics of our own. The strategic achievement would be more demanding: a continent sufficiently confident to show the world its real dimensions, publish its real weaknesses, measure its real potential and correct its own mistakes in public.

## 17. THE PEN BELONGS IN AFRICAN HANDS. SO DOES THE BURDEN OF PROOF.

The UN has recognised the case for a fairer map. That victory now confronts African governments with a harder assignment. Map the households that are not in the register. Map the farms that are not connected to markets. Map the workshops that are invisible to industrial policy. Verify the minerals. Reconcile the exports. Measure the skills. Publish the uncertainty. Correct the mistakes. Invite scrutiny.

When writing the story of your life, do not allow anybody else to hold the pen. But do not forget what makes that story credible: evidence, not indignation; independent measurement, not official assertion; accountable institutions, not another generation of slogans.

> **Africa does not merely need to correct the map. Africa must acquire the capacity, discipline and courage to correct the data.**
