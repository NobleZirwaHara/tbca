## Access is the opening line, not the conclusion

A bank account is useful. It is not the same as financial security. A person may have an account but still be unable to save safely, pay a nearby merchant, obtain fair credit or withstand a medical emergency. For policymakers and financial-service providers, the distinction between ownership and effective usage is central to evaluating inclusion.

The World Bank’s Global Findex 2025, drawing on 2024 surveys, reported that formal savings in Sub-Saharan Africa rose by twelve percentage points to 35% of adults \[1\]. The same release reported that 40% of adults in developing economies saved in a financial account in 2024, an increase of sixteen percentage points since 2021. The two figures cover different populations and should not be combined as though they represent the same denominator.

Technology has helped. The World Bank reported that 10% of adults in developing economies used mobile-money accounts to save in 2024, five percentage points more than in 2021 \[1\]. But the existence of a mobile-money service does not guarantee affordable data, reliable connectivity, accessible agents or protection against fraud. The distance between the customer and the system is not only geographical. It can be linguistic, financial and institutional.

When a financial institution reports millions of accounts opened, ask the harder questions. How many are active? What is the median balance? How often can customers transact without failure? What do they pay in fees relative to income? Can they resolve a mistaken transfer? A dormant account may improve a dashboard more quickly than it improves a household. Financial inclusion requires measurement after registration day.

## Four frictions that keep people out

The first friction is identity and onboarding. People without easily verifiable documentation, consistent addresses or digital records may struggle to satisfy account-opening requirements. Solutions must preserve financial-integrity obligations while reducing unnecessary complexity. The second is affordability. Fees, transport, airtime and lost time can make a nominally low-cost account expensive to use.

The third is product mismatch. A farmer with seasonal income does not need the same repayment schedule as a salaried employee. A market trader may value instant settlement and a simple transaction history more than a sophisticated mobile interface. The fourth is trust. Unexpected deductions, confusing terms, scams and slow dispute resolution deter adoption even where branches or agents exist.

The Global Findex survey covered roughly 148,000 adults in 141 economies and collected information about accounts, payments, borrowing, saving, connectivity and digital safety \[2\]. This breadth matters because the policy problem is multidimensional. An account-ownership trend alone cannot reveal whether households became more resilient or whether women, young people and rural users share the gains.

Kenya’s experience with mobile money is an instructive real-world example of innovation shaped around everyday payment needs. It demonstrates the importance of distribution, simple use cases and a trusted transaction system, not the inevitability that copying an app will reproduce identical outcomes elsewhere. Regulatory institutions, agent networks, affordability and consumer behaviour differ by market. Copy the analytical discipline before copying the interface.

## Financial health is the missing outcome measure

Account activity can increase without an improvement in household resilience. Users may transact more frequently because merchants accept digital payments, but still be unable to absorb a financial shock. Evaluate savings consistency, access to emergency funds, debt stress and the fairness of product terms where measurement is appropriate and consented to. The relevant outcomes should be selected with the target population, not imposed by a product team alone.

Financial-service providers must also plan for failures. What happens when a mobile network is unavailable, an agent has no liquidity, or a customer loses a handset? Design recovery processes, reliable transaction confirmations, transparent dispute records and human support for complex complaints. Financial trust is often won or lost on the day the system behaves badly.

The commercial argument need not oppose inclusion. Low-value payments, responsible savings products and reliable merchant services can support sustainable businesses if costs are controlled and customers benefit. A provider should demonstrate both: viable unit economics and meaningful customer value. Neither is credible without actual transaction and service evidence.

## Build inclusion around useful financial lives

Begin with a customer-journey map for distinct groups: informal merchants, youth entering employment, rural households and microenterprises. For each group, identify the top three transactions they need to complete, the failure points and the total customer cost. Test services in the language and device environment people actually use. Provide assisted channels for customers who are not digitally confident.

Design credit with affordability tests and clear disclosure. Use transaction data responsibly, obtain legally valid permissions and provide mechanisms to contest automated decisions. Measure complaints, fraud loss, over-indebtedness and service uptime alongside customer acquisition. A loan that increases volume while leaving people trapped in unaffordable debt should not be presented as a triumph of inclusion.

Governments and regulators can help by promoting interoperable payment infrastructure, proportional risk-based onboarding and effective consumer redress. Financial institutions can invest in agent reliability, transparent pricing, savings design and appropriate products. Employers and value-chain buyers can improve inclusion through reliable digital payments and faster settlement to workers and small suppliers.

A practical scoreboard needs five measures: active usage, affordability, successful transactions, resilience and customer redress. Evaluate each by relevant customer group to avoid averages concealing exclusion. Use Findex trends as a useful baseline, then commission local evidence before scaling a product. “Everyone has an app” is not a strategy if half the intended users cannot complete a transaction or recover a disputed payment.

## Boardroom takeaway

Reward providers for sustained, safe usage and improved financial outcomes, not only account registrations. Inclusion succeeds when customers can use the service on a difficult Tuesday, not merely on a promotional launch day.
