## First, stop confusing exposure with unemployment

The title is deliberately provocative. It is not a labour-market forecast. The International Labour Organization’s refined 2025 assessment found that roughly one in four workers globally held jobs with some exposure to generative AI, while 3.3% of global employment fell into its highest exposure category \[1\]. Exposure measures the technical potential to automate tasks. It does not mean those jobs have disappeared or that they will.

For African businesses, the immediate question is less dramatic and more useful: which repetitive activities consume skilled time without improving decisions? Think about first-draft proposals, document classification, meeting-note synthesis, invoice matching and customer-service triage. These are plausible areas for assistance when data, process rules and review steps are well defined. Complex negotiations, fiduciary decisions and context-sensitive advice still require accountable people.

A consulting firm can use AI to compare a draft proposal against a tender’s requirements, flag missing evidence and organise a source register. But if the system invents a customer reference or an unsupported revenue forecast, the firm remains accountable. A machine can generate fluent nonsense at industrial speed. That is not a competitive advantage unless the competitors are equally careless.

The ILO also warns that outcomes depend on investment, training and workplace choices \[1\]. The strategic issue is therefore adoption design, not a contest between humans and machines. Successful teams combine domain expertise, workflow discipline, reliable information and structured checking. Buying subscriptions before selecting useful tasks reverses that sequence.

## Start with workflows, not tool catalogues

Select ten recurring tasks. Score each for frequency, time spent, error cost, data sensitivity and ease of validation. Prioritise work that is frequent, measurable and low-risk. For example, a first-pass customer enquiry classification can be tested against a labelled historical sample. A decision to deny credit to an applicant cannot be treated as a convenient experiment with an opaque output.

Build the task as a controlled process: defined inputs; a specific instruction; access to approved documents; output in a consistent format; and a named human reviewer. Measure precision, turnaround time, rework and the cost of oversight. If a five-minute task requires fifteen minutes of correction, the automation may have successfully increased productivity in the opposite direction.

Keep sensitive data within approved systems. Verify service contracts, retention rules, cross-border transfers and sector-specific duties before uploading personal or commercially confidential information. AI governance is not a separate annual seminar; it belongs in the ordinary controls for procurement, information security, quality and audit.

The ILO’s 2025 analysis identifies clerical occupations among those with the highest exposure \[1\]. That makes clerical workflows sensible candidates for task redesign, but it also increases the obligation to retrain people and change job descriptions thoughtfully. Managers should distinguish elimination of an inefficient task from elimination of a worker’s future. Staff are far more likely to help improve a workflow when the incentive is not a surprise redundancy letter.

## What a sensible human-in-the-loop rule looks like

Not every automated output needs the same control. Put low-risk drafting and internal classification in a supervised productivity tier. Put customer-facing recommendations and material financial calculations in a reviewed tier. Put decisions that affect rights, regulatory compliance or irreversible commitments behind explicit accountable human approval.

Document the dataset used to test a workflow, the expected failure modes and the review frequency. For example, test a proposed invoice-matching assistant on previously reconciled transactions with known exceptions. Report false positives as well as accurate matches. A system that achieves impressive average accuracy but misses high-value exceptions can be commercially unsuitable.

Training should cover source checking, prompt injection risks, confidential data handling and the temptation to treat polished prose as proof. Establish an incident path for wrong outputs and periodic retesting whenever the workflow or model changes. Adoption succeeds when staff know when to trust the tool, when to check it and when to refuse its recommendation.

## A ninety-day adoption scorecard

In the first month, set a baseline. Count hours, defects and cost per completed activity. Adopt one approved use case and name an accountable owner. In the second month, test with real but appropriately protected data; retain a control sample completed without AI; and have reviewers record accuracy, omissions and unsupported statements. Do not evaluate the system only by whether the prose sounds confident.

In the third month, connect the proven process to the actual workflow. Train users on exceptions and escalation. Track four figures weekly: cycle time, first-time-right rate, verified net hours saved and incidents. Include licensing and supervision costs in the calculation. Where errors affect customers or rights, require explicit human review before release.

An illustrative metric helps. If twenty staff members each reclaim two verified hours per week, that is forty hours before review and integration costs. At a hypothetical loaded cost of R300 per hour, the gross capacity equivalent is R12,000 per week. This is not necessarily a cash saving: employees may use the time to improve service instead of reducing expenditure. Calling capacity “profit” is how cheerful business cases become awkward audits.

The durable advantage is not that a team can type faster. It is that the organisation learns to combine trusted data, sound judgement and repeatable execution. The best AI project may be the one that frees a senior professional to ask a better question.

## Boardroom takeaway

Fund three measured pilot workflows, not fifty unmanaged subscriptions. Require an owner, baseline, validation protocol, privacy review and human approval point for each.
