## The five pillars are a management system, not a motivational poster

Execution is sometimes described as grit, as though failed projects are mainly a shortage of inspiring speeches. In reality, many initiatives fail because no one has made the essential decisions: who is accountable, which resources are available, what must happen first, how success will be measured and what happens when the facts change. The BusinessClinic.Africa proposes five pillars for practical use: choice, ownership, capacity, evidence and correction.

This is an editorial operating framework, not a claimed quotation from a named founder and not a certified external methodology. Its purpose is to give boards and management a repeatable diagnostic. A useful framework should survive contact with month-end reporting, suppliers, customers and the unpleasant discovery that the original cost estimate was wrong.

The World Bank’s Business Ready 2025 assessment covers 101 economies and distinguishes regulatory rules, public-service provision and operational efficiency \[1\]. The comparison is instructive. Companies also need to separate announced intentions, resources placed in service and results achieved. An approved strategy is not a delivered service. Nobody ever received a functioning water connection from a beautifully typeset committee resolution.

## Pillars one and two: choice and ownership

Pillar one, strategic choice, means defining the result, customer or beneficiary, commercial rationale and explicit exclusions. A logistics business targeting reduced failed deliveries must decide which routes, contracts and causes it will address first. If management adds five unrelated initiatives “for completeness”, it has reduced the chance of completing any of them. Good prioritisation is a statement about what will not receive capacity.

Pillar two, ownership, requires one accountable executive, one delivery owner and a documented decision path. The executive removes obstacles and authorises trade-offs; the delivery owner coordinates daily execution. The board receives independent performance information but does not become the operational help desk. Agree escalation thresholds before trouble arises: for example, a forecast overspend above 10% or a critical legal approval delayed by thirty days triggers a decision forum. These thresholds are proposed design choices, not industry averages.

Every milestone must have acceptance criteria. “Platform ready” is not enough. Specify security testing complete, customer data migrated, staff trained, payment reconciliations working and controlled live transactions passed. The phrase “almost done” should never have unlimited diplomatic immunity.

## Pillars three and four: capacity and evidence

Pillar three, delivery capacity, covers people, cash, suppliers, equipment, permissions and data. Most serious initiatives have at least one shared bottleneck. A company may have four project managers but only one engineer able to sign off safety-critical design. Creating five parallel projects does not create five engineers. Maintain a capacity map, identify the constrained resource and resequence work around reality.

Pillar four, evidence, requires observable outputs and outcome measures. Separate activity from result. Training 200 employees is an activity; passing a competency assessment is evidence of capability; reducing rework and customer complaints is evidence of operational benefit. A dashboard should carry owner, current value, baseline, target, measurement date and source system. If nobody can trace a number to source records, classify it as an estimate.

The G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance 2023 require attention to strategy, risk, management oversight and credible disclosure \[2\]. Their value here is a governance discipline: boards should inspect performance evidence and downside exposure, not manage by adjectives. A dashboard filled entirely with reassuring green circles may be reporting the organisation’s appetite for discomfort rather than its operational condition.

## Pillar five: correction and the 90-day operating rhythm

Pillar five, correction, means using evidence to continue, change, stop or escalate. Weekly reviews should resolve obstacles, not merely collect status updates. Monthly reviews should test cash use, risk movement and benefits. Quarterly board reviews should decide whether the investment thesis remains valid. Stop-loss rules are especially important when sunk costs encourage leaders to continue because “we have already spent so much.” Money already spent is not a reason to spend the next rand badly.

Run the framework across ninety days. In days 1–15 rank the portfolio, cut duplicate work and name owners. In days 16–30 verify capacity and establish baselines. In days 31–60 deliver small, independently tested outputs. In days 61–90 evaluate outcomes, remediate bottlenecks and redirect resources. Publish no more than a one-page scorecard for each initiative: choice, owner, capacity, proof, correction required.

For a quick maturity check, score each pillar from zero to four: absent, documented, operating, measured or independently validated. The maximum is twenty. This scoring scheme is an internal editorial tool, not an external certification. A project with 16/20 may still be unviable if its one missing pillar is legal permission. Arithmetic should support judgement, not replace it.

## Worked application: an SME service expansion

Consider a medium-sized service provider planning to open operations in a second province. The strategic choice is to enter only two customer segments for which it has evidence of repeat demand. Ownership is assigned to a commercial sponsor with budget authority and an operating manager accountable for the launch. Capacity is tested through staff availability, local partners, support coverage and the ability to collect payments. Evidence is defined as paying customers, successful service completions and cash collected within agreed terms. Correction rules specify when to change pricing, narrow scope or stop.

Assume the expansion has an approved R2.4 million launch envelope. Management proposes releasing R600,000 for a pilot and the balance in three subsequent tranches only if delivery and collections meet thresholds. Each tranche is R600,000, so the arithmetic is R2.4 million divided by four. This is an illustrative governance structure, not an investment-return forecast or prescribed funding model. It makes the next payment dependent on learning rather than on the optimism of the original presentation.

The first customer cohort may prove that service quality is strong while acquisition costs are higher than expected. Under a weak execution model management celebrates the positive indicator and ignores the loss. Under the five-pillar model the owner reports both facts and proposes a decision: improve the channel, revisit price or constrain expansion. The review is successful because it changes the resource allocation before losses multiply, not because the original plan remained intact.

This approach is adaptable to a hospital improvement, a municipal customer-service process or a construction project. Change the metrics and legal gates to fit the sector. Do not change the need for ownership, capacity, proof and correction. On every operating dashboard, show the next critical decision and the evidence required to make it. Without a decision, the scorecard is only attractive office stationery.

## Boardroom takeaway

**Require a named owner, a credible baseline, a measurable improvement and a decision date. Revisit the evidence before committing the next tranche of time, money or reputation.**
