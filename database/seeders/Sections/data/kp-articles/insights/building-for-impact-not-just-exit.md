## What does building for impact really mean?

Question: Is an exit automatically the wrong ambition? Answer: No. Investors deserve liquidity, founders deserve choices and employees may benefit from a well-managed ownership transition. The error is making a valuation event the only definition of success. A company can produce a spectacular exit and leave behind weak suppliers, an exhausted workforce and customers still looking for the promised service. Likewise, a founder can refuse every exit and trap employees in a business that cannot raise the capital it needs. The relevant test is whether value survives a change in ownership.

Question: What evidence distinguishes durable impact from corporate poetry? Answer: Track the outcome that the enterprise is supposed to create. For a clinic, this might be patients treated safely and affordably. For a logistics firm, it might be reliable delivery and measurable supplier participation. For a financing platform, it is not merely the number of loans approved but repayment quality, borrower outcomes and responsible pricing. Announcing that the business is “transforming lives” without defining whose lives, and how they changed, is a communications exercise, not impact measurement.

The World Bank’s October 2025 Africa’s Pulse reported that wage-paying jobs accounted for only 24% of employment in Sub-Saharan Africa, with a smaller share when Southern Africa is excluded \[1\]. That regional statistic does not tell us whether any particular company creates good jobs. It does, however, explain why an African business should pay attention to job quality, training progression and supplier development rather than treating headcount as a ceremonial trophy.

## Can impact and financial performance coexist?

Question: Does impact require sacrificing returns? Answer: Sometimes there are trade-offs, and pretending otherwise insults everyone who has ever managed a budget. A business may choose to invest in safer equipment, accessible customer service or technical training before those investments raise near-term margins. The commercial question is whether the expenditure creates resilient operations, customer trust, lower turnover or a licence to operate. Management should model the cost and the benefit rather than declaring that every noble intention is automatically profitable.

IFC describes micro, small and medium enterprises as more than 90% of firms globally and as accounting on average for about 70% of employment \[2\]. These global estimates are not a measurement of one country or one sector. They show why improving smaller firms’ productivity and survival can matter alongside the headline fortunes of the biggest firms. An impact thesis should therefore include the strength of local suppliers, not just the visibility of the lead company.

Question: What about philanthropy? Answer: It is legitimate, but it should not be used to distract from harmful core operations. A company that funds a small community event while consistently paying suppliers late may have mastered public relations. It has not mastered responsible enterprise. Start with fair contracts, safe work, reliable customer outcomes and disciplined governance. Community investment is additional, not an invoice that cancels weak conduct.

## What would a responsible founder measure?

Question: Which numbers belong in the board pack? Answer: Put commercial health beside value created. Show cash conversion, customer retention, product quality, staff turnover, supplier payment terms, safety outcomes and any specifically claimed social or environmental results. Record a baseline, target, measurement period and evidence owner. Disaggregate where relevant and lawful. A company cannot claim improved livelihoods merely because a payment passed through its bank account.

The G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance 2023 devote a chapter to sustainability and resilience and make clear that boards oversee strategy, risk and disclosure \[3\]. Those principles are guidance rather than a substitute for domestic company law. Still, they provide a useful test: has the board discussed long-term risks with the same seriousness as this quarter’s revenue? If the only sustainability metric is the number of pages in the annual report, the answer may be apparent.

A sensible example is a digital lender. Management could report active customers and fees, but it should also measure complaints resolved, transparent pricing, arrears by vintage and whether borrowers are re-borrowing to service prior debt. A rising loan book is not necessarily progress. Sometimes the numbers are applauding a problem.

## The decision every owner eventually faces

Question: How should a founder prepare for succession or a sale? Answer: Document processes, build leadership beyond the founder, reconcile financial statements, protect intellectual property, clarify ownership and record customer contracts. These are not just exit documents. They make the business stronger today. Then evaluate potential buyers against continuity, workforce impact and credible investment capacity as well as price.

Question: What is the one practical step to take this month? Answer: Identify the three stakeholders whose outcomes determine the company’s social licence: customers, workers and suppliers, or the relevant equivalents. Ask each group for evidence of what works and what fails. Select three measurable improvements, assign an executive owner and report the results at the next board meeting. Do not commission a new slogan until someone has improved an actual service.

The enduring point is simple: a strong exit is one possible reward for building well; it is not proof that the business made a useful difference.

## An evidence pact for the next twelve months

**A founder who wants to build for the long term can start with an impact ledger that the finance director is willing to sign. Define who benefits, how the benefit occurs, what could make it fail and what data can reasonably be collected. Separate outputs such as “people enrolled” from outcomes such as “people employed six months later.” For a supplier-development programme, count suppliers that meet quality and delivery standards and remain commercially active, not just those invited to training. Disclose the measurement window and any missing records.**

Create a simple business-and-impact scorecard. One column tracks commercial sustainability: gross margin, operating cash conversion, repeat revenue and regulatory compliance. A second tracks claimed outcomes: customer service performance, skills completed and verified supplier payments, where relevant. A third tracks harms and trade-offs: product complaints, workplace incidents, pollution incidents or overdue supplier balances. There is no perfect universal score. The discipline is to make trade-offs visible and prevent the attractive measures from hiding the inconvenient ones.

Ask an external reviewer to challenge one material claim each year. If a company says its financing improves enterprise survival, verify whether customer businesses survive at a higher rate than a credible comparison, and state the limits of the comparison. If it cannot make such a claim, report what it knows instead. Account ownership, transaction volume and disbursements are not sufficient evidence of increased welfare. They are valid operational measures, but they answer different questions.

At the annual strategy review, ask whether the organisation would remain valuable if the founder were unavailable for ninety days. That test identifies succession gaps, unrecorded relationships and undocumented judgement. The strongest impact strategy may be the unglamorous transfer of capability into the institution. A business that only works when its founder is in the room has built dependence, not resilience.

## Boardroom takeaway

**Require a named owner, a credible baseline, a measurable improvement and a decision date. Revisit the evidence before committing the next tranche of time, money or reputation.**
