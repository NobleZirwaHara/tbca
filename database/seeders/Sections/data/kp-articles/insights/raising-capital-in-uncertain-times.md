## The capital market is not obliged to like your timing

Entrepreneurs sometimes describe finance as if a shortage of banknotes is the only obstacle. But investors may be holding capital and still refuse a specific deal because cash flows are uncertain, governance is weak, security is inadequate or valuations have not adjusted to risk. In uncertain conditions the price of patience rises. A confident founder can still be valuable; confidence unsupported by contracts is expensive advertising.

There is real evidence of financing activity alongside real scarcity. The World Bank’s Private Participation in Infrastructure database reports that investment commitments in low- and middle-income countries reached US\$100.7 billion in 2024, up 16% from 2023 \[1\]. Those are infrastructure commitments across covered countries, not the amount of capital available to African SMEs or proof that lending conditions are easy. The number illustrates a distinction: deals close where risks, counterparties and expected cash flows can be structured.

IFC’s September 2025 announcement of a US\$100 million facility for FirstRand Bank to support smaller firms in South Africa also reported that only about 5% of formal MSMEs had access to credit, citing MSME Finance Gap research \[2\]. That estimate has a specific definition and research basis; it should not be casually extended to all informal businesses. Its implication for entrepreneurs is nevertheless serious. Demand alone does not make a transaction financeable.

## What lenders and investors are protecting

A lender asks: what funds scheduled repayments after interest, costs and adverse demand? An equity investor asks: what creates enterprise value, how much dilution is justified and how could returns eventually be realised? A grant maker tests eligible outcomes and conditions. A blended-finance provider may accept a different risk or return because its mandate is different. Confusing these instruments creates unnecessary rejection and occasionally a capital structure that breaks the business even when the product works.

Build a defensible funding ask around the specific use of proceeds. Identify the milestone financed, the resulting revenue or risk reduction, timing, collateral or protections, costs, investor rights and alternative if the milestone slips. A R20 million request without a sources-and-uses schedule is not an investment case. It is an amount typed in bold.

Price downside honestly. Use at least three scenarios and state which assumptions change. Calculate cash runway from cash on hand plus committed inflows minus unavoidable outflows. For a business with R6 million available cash and net burn of R750,000 per month, simple runway is eight months (R6 million divided by R750,000). This illustrative calculation excludes working-capital surprises and does not establish creditworthiness.

## Reduce risk before negotiating price

First, remove avoidable uncertainty. Reconcile management accounts with banking records and tax submissions. Separate contracted revenue from prospects. Show current customer concentration, churn, gross margin and collections by age. Document legal rights, intellectual property, permits, key supplier dependencies and beneficial ownership. A credible data room is not a decorative Dropbox containing yesterday’s pitch deck.

Second, match instrument and maturity. Use receivables finance only against eligible, verifiable debtor quality. Align long-lived capital expenditure with funding tenors and a realistic ramp-up period. Do not fund a two-year construction programme using repayable finance that matures before commissioning unless another committed facility covers the gap. For a young technology venture with volatile cash generation, staged equity may be structurally more appropriate than aggressive amortisation.

Third, seek risk sharing where feasible. Advance payments, purchase orders with enforceable terms, insurance, guarantees, staged disbursements, supplier credit and credible co-investors can each address specific risks. IFC’s 2025 MSME finance overview describes extensive institutional lending and a continued financing gap \[3\]. The lesson is not that one instrument solves every case. It is that different risks require different capital mechanisms.

## The negotiation that actually matters

Founders obsess over valuation and interest rate because they fit easily into conversation. Review the rest of the term sheet: covenants, security, reporting deadlines, drawdown conditions, conversion rights, board controls, permitted distributions, default remedies and currency mismatch. A seemingly cheap loan can be costly if covenant triggers are unrealistic. A high headline equity valuation can conceal onerous liquidation preferences.

Set a 30-day readiness sprint. Days 1–7 reconcile facts and material risks. Days 8–14 build the model and test customer proof. Days 15–21 prepare a data room and legal gap list. Days 22–30 run an investor-style challenge session and adjust the financing structure. Ask a sceptical colleague to identify the three assumptions that could destroy repayment or expected returns. That colleague may be the most useful person at the meeting.

Capital will not always be available on acceptable terms. Sometimes the correct response is to shrink scope, preserve cash, strengthen receivables or postpone expansion. The strategic objective is not to win the biggest cheque. It is to finance a business without sacrificing the conditions under which that business can survive.

## A credible capital story in four documents

The first document is a two-page investment thesis: customer problem, verified demand, competitive advantage, unit economics, use of funds and the decision sought. The second is a reconciled financial model that makes all material assumptions visible. The third is a risk-and-mitigation schedule covering revenue concentration, currency mismatch, legal rights, key people and execution milestones. The fourth is the data-room index that lets a lender or investor verify every major claim. A longer deck cannot compensate for missing supporting evidence.

Use cash-flow scenarios that reflect what could genuinely change. If the base case assumes twelve customers paying within thirty days, a downside case might assume eight paying after sixty days with operating expenses unchanged. Show whether the resulting cash shortfall requires a longer facility, a smaller initial launch, equity support or revised customer terms. Avoid optimistic inputs that only change colours in a sensitivity table without changing the investment decision.

Govern the fundraising process itself. Agree who speaks for the business, what information may be shared, which terms require board approval and how investor feedback is recorded. Compare term sheets on a common basis: funding certainty, net proceeds after fees, dilution, effective cost, repayment schedule, governance rights, currency and termination rights. The cheapest number on page one may conceal the most restrictive clause on page seventeen.

A disciplined founder knows when not to accept money. A facility secured against essential assets with unrealistic repayment assumptions can destroy a healthy operating business. An equity investor whose governance rights prevent necessary operational decisions may be equally unsuitable. Fundraising succeeds when financing and enterprise needs are aligned, not merely when the announcement attracts applause.

## Boardroom takeaway

**Require a named owner, a credible baseline, a measurable improvement and a decision date. Revisit the evidence before committing the next tranche of time, money or reputation.**
