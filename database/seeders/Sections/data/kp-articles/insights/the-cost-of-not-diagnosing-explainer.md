## The expensive comfort of the obvious answer

A sales decline arrives. Management hires more salespeople. Margins contract. Management imposes a blanket cost cut. Customers complain. Management sends frontline employees on another training course. These interventions can be appropriate. They can also fail spectacularly when the underlying issue is a broken proposition, a pricing error, unreliable service or a customer journey designed by someone who has apparently never been a customer.

Diagnosis means distinguishing the observable symptom from the mechanism that produces it. Revenue is not a diagnosis. Neither is employee morale, a high debtor balance or slow project delivery. These are signals. Ask when the problem appeared, whom it affects, what changed immediately before it, what evidence contradicts the obvious story and how an intervention could make things worse.

The World Bank’s Business Ready 2025 framework explicitly separates the quality of regulation, the provision of public services and operational efficiency \[1\]. It does so because writing a rule, offering a service and making that service work are different propositions. Business leaders face the same problem internally. A policy may be excellent, the software expensive and the operating outcome dreadful. Buying another policy will not repair the workflow.

## Put a price on repeating the wrong treatment

Take an illustrative distributor with R40 million in annual sales. Its customer refunds and repeat deliveries cost 2% of revenue, or R800,000 each year. A new CRM package costing R300,000 is approved because complaints are rising. A root-cause review later shows that the product catalogue and dispatch codes disagree. The CRM improves complaint visibility but does not fix dispatch accuracy. The annual R800,000 leakage continues. This calculation is an example, not a measured company case: R40,000,000 × 0.02 = R800,000.

The cost of misdiagnosis has at least four components: money spent on unsuitable remedies, losses sustained while the actual cause continues, opportunity cost of delayed improvement and the damage done to credibility when another “turnaround” fails. Some costs are measurable; others require assumptions. Keep them separate. Never create a precise-looking savings claim out of vague executive frustration.

Before approving a programme, establish the baseline. Which cohorts are affected? Is the failure clustered around one product, region or process step? Are there seasonal effects or new regulations? Does the issue predate the current manager? Where possible compare similar units rather than relying on recollection. Anecdotes start investigations; they do not conclude them.

## A six-stage diagnosis that management can use

First, write the problem as an observable variance: “On-time delivery fell from 94% to 81% between January and March for route group B.” Do not write “logistics leadership is weak.” Second, map the process from customer request to final outcome. Third, gather source evidence such as timestamped transactions, error codes, interviews and financial records. Fourth, test competing hypotheses rather than confirming the preferred story.

Fifth, run a contained experiment. Fix one suspected cause in a controlled location, compare the result with a credible baseline and document unintended effects. Sixth, scale only after verification, with named owners and an exit rule. A project with no hypothesis, no baseline and no test is not a transformation programme. It is an expensive expression of optimism.

A related lesson emerges from McKinsey’s 2025 global AI survey. Among its respondents, redesigning workflows was the organisational change most associated with reported EBIT impact from generative AI \[2\]. This is survey evidence and correlation, not proof that any particular technology intervention will increase profit. The strategic lesson is narrower and sounder: tools cannot compensate automatically for a badly specified process.

## Governance: the question that saves the next quarter

Create a one-page diagnostic record with six fields: symptom, baseline, suspected causes, evidence, experiment and decision threshold. An executive sponsor signs the hypothesis before spending is authorised. A finance partner validates the cost model. A frontline employee tests whether the proposed fix is operationally credible. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It stops the next consultant, vendor or department from redefining the problem to fit its preferred product.

Review the intervention after thirty, sixty and ninety days. If the expected leading indicators do not move, pause expansion and revisit the cause. The board should celebrate the early discovery of a wrong hypothesis because it saves later capital. Most organisations claim to value learning, right until learning requires admitting that the approved PowerPoint was mistaken.

The decisive question is not “What can we buy to make the problem disappear?” It is “What evidence would convince us that we understand why it happens?” Good diagnosis is not glamorous. Neither is paying to fix the same failure three times.

Consider a practical cash-collection diagnosis. Management sees debtors above sixty days and blames the credit controller. A transaction review shows that a substantial portion of late balances consists of disputed invoices with incorrect purchase-order references. The apparent collections problem is at least partly an order-entry and billing-quality problem. Hiring an additional collector could increase calls without accelerating settlement. The correct test is the proportion of disputed invoices, average time to resolution, days-sales-outstanding by customer cohort and the share of invoices accepted on first submission.

Build a two-by-two decision map. One axis is financial loss if the hypothesis is wrong; the other is the ease of testing the hypothesis. Test low-cost, high-consequence assumptions first. For example, sample twenty disputed invoices and compare the original orders with the issued bills before commissioning a new enterprise system. If the evidence is mixed, segment by customer, product and responsible process. The exercise is not statistically representative merely because the sample includes twenty records. Define the population and sampling logic before drawing general conclusions.

Finally, audit the decision process itself. How often did last quarter’s remedial projects define a baseline? How many had a named hypothesis, a control comparison and a review date? How many stopped when the results contradicted expectations? The answers reveal whether the organisation is learning or repeatedly purchasing certainty it does not possess. In business diagnostics the most expensive sentence is often “we already know what the problem is.” That claim is sometimes true. It should still be tested before the purchase order is signed.

## A decision rule that protects scarce management attention

Before approving an expensive remedy, require a five-line investment note. Line one states the measurable symptom. Line two lists at least two plausible causes, including one that would contradict the sponsor’s preferred solution. Line three lists the evidence needed to distinguish them. Line four estimates the cost of testing. Line five defines a stop or expand threshold. This note is deliberately smaller than a conventional business case. Its purpose is to prevent months of elaboration around an untested assumption.

Not every issue justifies a lengthy study. A safety incident, data leak or legal breach may require immediate containment before diagnosis is complete. In those cases act first to protect people and the enterprise, preserve evidence and then investigate the causes systematically. For ordinary performance gaps, by contrast, the cost of a short test is often smaller than the cost of rushing to a permanent system replacement. Distinguish urgent containment from durable correction.

The board should ask how many previously approved remedies achieved the outcomes promised in their business cases, using the same measurement definitions and observation windows. If those records do not exist, the organisation has a diagnosis problem about its diagnosis process. Building the discipline now is cheaper than repeating another programme with a new name and identical underlying assumptions.

## Boardroom takeaway

**Require a named owner, a credible baseline, a measurable improvement and a decision date. Revisit the evidence before committing the next tranche of time, money or reputation.**
