## The ceremonial launch is not a feasibility study

A board approves a project. A photograph is taken. Somebody announces a completion date. Only afterwards does the team ask who owns the land, whether the utility connection exists and how much the permits will cost. This is not mobilisation. It is a very public way of discovering what should have been established privately.

The scale of avoidable trouble is documented. In its 2024–25 national and provincial audit, South Africa’s Auditor-General reviewed 152 infrastructure projects with a combined estimated cost of R47.39 billion and identified findings on 136 projects, or 89% of the sample \[1\]. That statistic applies to the audited sample, not every project in South Africa. Nevertheless, it is a warning to anyone who treats preparation as optional overhead.

Project failure often begins as an unresolved assumption. Demand is forecast from population rather than paying customers. A capital estimate omits grid connections and escalation. The site is “secured” by a letter that does not establish enforceable rights. The delivery model assumes specialist contractors will somehow be available at the preferred price. These assumptions then become the financial model, the board submission and finally an inconvenient reality.

It is important to distinguish four decisions: whether the idea is attractive, whether it is feasible, whether the business case is investable and whether the project is ready to execute. Each decision requires different evidence. Skipping the middle gates creates a false sense of progress. A project can have social value and still be technically unbuildable on the proposed site. It can be technically feasible and still have economics that do not support private debt.

## Five failures hidden in the preparation phase

Failure one is a weak problem definition. If the sponsor cannot define beneficiaries, paying customers, baseline conditions and measurable success, it cannot credibly select a solution.

Failure two is false cost confidence. A single point estimate, without quantities, contingencies or price-risk allocation, is not financial certainty. It is a number waiting for an argument.

Failure three is unresolved rights and approvals. Land, water, environmental and sector permissions are not “paperwork”; they are schedule and bankability constraints. Failure four is poor risk allocation. A supplier cannot reasonably bear risks it cannot control, and a sponsor should not pretend that transferring a risk into a contract makes the risk disappear. Failure five is governance drift. When nobody has authority to stop a weak proposal, optimism becomes policy.

The Auditor-General’s 2024–25 municipal audit reported findings on 101 of 129 infrastructure projects assessed and an average project delay of 25 months for the reviewed group \[2\]. These outcomes reflect public-sector delivery, and should not be used as a predicted delay for any individual commercial project. Their practical message is wider: approvals, procurement, competent contractors and contract administration need to work as one system.

An effective project gate asks six questions before procurement begins: Is the need evidenced? Is the solution technically fit for purpose? Are the site and statutory pathways verified? Does the model withstand plausible downside cases? Is funding matched to a realistic construction and operating schedule? Who can stop the project when evidence changes? A confident “yes” requires a document, a responsible person and a recent validation date.

## The preparation budget is a risk-control budget

Sponsors are sometimes willing to spend tens of millions on construction but reluctant to authorise independent geotechnical testing or a detailed market study. That trade-off deserves scrutiny. The relevant question is not whether preparation is cheap. It is whether its expected value exceeds the downside loss it may prevent.

As an illustration, consider a R100 million project with a plausible unresolved site risk that could impose R12 million of remedial cost. A R600,000 investigation that can meaningfully resolve the uncertainty may be defensible even if it does not produce a construction drawing. The figures are hypothetical and do not establish a universal project-preparation budget.

Prepare a stage-by-stage evidence matrix: concept, pre-feasibility, full feasibility, investment approval and procurement readiness. Identify the deliverable that closes each uncertainty and the decision that follows. If the answer is “we will discover that once we start,” insist that management quantify the potential consequence and document why deferral is acceptable.

## Build an immunity system, not a thicker report

Start with a pre-feasibility screen. Compare alternatives, including the option to do less or do nothing. Next commission fit-for-purpose technical, environmental, market and legal studies, using specialists who can challenge the preferred answer. Then produce a bankable business case with integrated schedule, operating model, procurement strategy and risk allocation.

At the investment-approval gate, assemble a red-team panel. Invite a commercial reviewer to attack the price assumptions, a technical reviewer to question constructability and a lawyer to identify rights or approvals that could block notice to proceed. Require evidence of genuine customer demand, not merely expressions of enthusiasm. A polite challenge at concept stage is cheaper than a dispute in month eighteen.

Put each critical assumption into a log with four fields: evidence, owner, last tested date and consequence if wrong. Require a change-control committee to approve design or scope changes that alter cost, timing or benefits. Report on leading indicators such as permits secured, design maturity and contractor readiness, not only money spent. Expenditure is proof of activity, not proof of progress.

The practical discipline is visible in a stop/go table: green when the evidence is complete; amber when a gap has a funded and dated remedy; red when a critical uncertainty remains unresolved. A red item should prevent an unconditional commitment even when a senior executive is keen to cut a ribbon.

## Boardroom takeaway

No final investment decision without a verified scope, rights matrix, independently challenged budget, risk allocation, integrated schedule and benefits owner. Good preparation is not a delay to delivery. It is how delivery becomes plausible.
