## 1 \| The cheque is not an answer to every question

Some founders appear to believe that raising money is a substitute for learning how the business works. They arrive with a glowing vision, an ambitious valuation and a funding requirement that has been rounded to a pleasing number. Ask what the proceeds will buy, and the room suddenly becomes a seminar on Africa's potential. Africa's potential is real. It is also not a cash-flow statement.

Before capital moves, a founder must answer a small number of difficult questions in plain language. Who is the customer? Why do they pay? What does it cost to serve them? What precisely must happen before the company can generate a return? Who has the authority and capability to deliver? What could make the investment fail, and who bears that loss? If the founder cannot answer these questions, the funder is being asked to purchase uncertainty without even receiving an accurate inventory of it.

Capital scarcity is genuine. IFC launched a global MSME financing platform in May 2024 with up to \$4 billion from its own account and an ambition to mobilise additional financing through participating financial institutions. That announcement is evidence of an institutional response to a significant financing constraint. It does not mean any specific applicant qualifies, or that financing is a universal remedy. \[1\]

The distinction is essential. A good company can be refused because the investor's mandate, risk appetite or timing does not fit. A weak company can be funded and still fail. Therefore, do not interpret a rejection as automatic proof that financiers are foolish, or an approval as proof your model is sound. Both are merely points in a longer evidence trail.

## 2 \| First answer: what problem are you being paid to solve?

Demand is not established by market size alone. "There are millions of potential customers" is a demographic observation, not a sales contract. Break the market into reachable buyers, describe the purchasing decision and show proof of willingness to pay. Interview losses as seriously as wins. Separate verbal enthusiasm from purchase orders, contingent letters from binding contracts and signed contracts from collected cash.

## 3 \| Second answer: where does the cash go?

A credible funding request has a uses-of-funds schedule connected to a timed operating plan. Distinguish development spending, capital assets, inventory, payroll, tax obligations, debt service, working-capital needs and a risk allowance. Show sources already committed and conditions still outstanding. A number appearing in a slide because it looked sufficiently ambitious is not a funding requirement. It is a design preference.

Build a base case, a downside case and a severe but plausible stress case. State revenue volumes, prices, gross margins, collection terms, cost escalation and completion dates explicitly. Explain how assumptions were obtained. If the largest customer pays three months late, which payments become difficult? If the project costs 15% more, what contingency, sponsor capital or scope adjustment applies? The 15% is an illustrative stress, not a prediction. Test sensitivity before someone who actually controls money tests it for you.

## 4 \| Third answer: who has already taken risk?

Investors reasonably ask what the sponsor has contributed, what remains at risk and whether the management team is competent to use additional capital. Sponsor contribution can include cash, assets, intellectual property or verifiable development expenditure, but do not disguise old sunk costs as cash available at financial close. Label each precisely. An attractive founder biography is not a project-completion guarantee.

The OECD's 2023 corporate governance principles emphasise material disclosure of financial position, ownership, risks and related-party transactions. The principles are designed as a corporate governance benchmark, not as a personal investment checklist. Yet the underlying demand is universal: funders need information that can be relied upon. \[2\] If ownership rights, legal claims or related-party contracts are complicated, present the complication before due diligence discovers it.

Treat transparency as a commercial capability. Prepare a consistent data room and document register. Make financial statements reconcile with management accounts and tax submissions. Explain litigation, licences, permits and security arrangements without euphemisms. The funder should not be forced to reconstruct the company from unexplained PDFs and three contradictory spreadsheets. That is not a treasure hunt anyone wants to finance.

## 5 \| Fourth answer: what is the route to a return?

Different instruments require different economics. A lender needs confidence that scheduled debt service can be met, with appropriate margin and security where required. An equity investor needs a plausible route to returns, governance rights and an exit or distribution logic. A grant provider may focus on impact, eligibility and measurable public value. Pitching identical material to all three is a reliable way to demonstrate that the financing strategy has not been thought through.

The intended return must be connected to actual activities. For a service business, show paid utilisation, delivery capacity, retention and customer concentration. For a manufacturing plant, demonstrate throughput, yield, energy, raw materials, maintenance and off-take. For a concession, show enforceable revenue rights, operating obligations, risk allocation and the regulatory environment. Numbers without a causal story are decoration. A causal story without numbers is a speech.

## 6 \| Fifth answer: what has to be true before you draw funds?

Develop a readiness gate. Corporate authority and ownership checked; land or site control confirmed where relevant; independent feasibility work commissioned; permits and approvals mapped; contracts classified as draft, conditional or binding; management team and controls assessed; model assumptions reconciled; funding structure matched to cash flow. Each outstanding item needs an accountable owner and a completion date. The purpose is to stop embarrassing surprises at the last moment.

Nigeria provides an important financing illustration. In December 2025, the World Bank announced a \$500 million FINCLUDE financing package to expand MSME finance, noting that fewer than one in twenty Nigerian MSMEs had access to bank credit. That is an institutional estimate and programme announcement, not a guarantee of access for an individual firm. \[3\] Finance barriers exist. So do underwriting requirements. Both realities deserve a place in the same discussion.

A founder should also know when not to take funding. Capital with covenants the business cannot meet, pricing that destroys economics or control terms inconsistent with the sponsor's objectives can become an expensive rescue that creates the next crisis. Refusing a bad deal can be an act of financial competence.

## 7 \| The questions for your investment committee rehearsal

Ask a colleague who is not in love with the opportunity to challenge the plan. What evidence would contradict the demand forecast? Which cost is least certain? What is the biggest contractual dependency? What happens if the first operating year disappoints? Who must sign which documents? Which licences are necessary before construction or trading? How long can the business operate if funding closes late? What would persuade a sceptical investor to walk away?

Write the answers in a decision memo, not merely on a presentation slide. Distinguish facts, estimates, assumptions and unresolved matters. Assign evidence grades. A paid invoice outranks an optimistic letter. A signed land agreement outranks a friendly conversation. An independent engineer's review outranks the founder's belief that the site "looks straightforward". Institutional investors may disagree about risks, but none benefits from discovering hidden ones accidentally.

## 8 \| Final provocation

The request for funding should be the final expression of readiness, not the opening substitute for preparation. A serious founder can tell a financier what is known, what remains uncertain, how the money will be controlled and how downside outcomes will be managed. That conversation invites judgement. It does not beg for applause.

Stop approaching capital providers as if your enthusiasm creates an obligation on their balance sheet. The next cheque belongs to someone who must explain its use to another person. Make that explanation possible. Before capital moves, answer the questions. If the answers are weak, the problem may not be the investor's lack of vision. It may be the venture's refusal to look carefully at itself.
